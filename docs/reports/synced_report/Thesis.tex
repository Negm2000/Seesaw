\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Newton's Second Law}

To accurately capture the system dynamics, the effective inertial load must account for both the physical mass of the assembly and the reflected rotational inertia of the motor armature. The total equivalent lumped mass $M_e$ translated along the rail is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	M_e = M_c + M_w + \frac{J_m K_g^2}{r_{mp}^2}
\end{equation}

Applying Newton’s Second Law to the translational motion of the cart, we account for the driving force $F_c$ and the resistive forces of friction. The equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
    F_c = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{eq} \cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $M_e$ is the total equivalent translational mass of the cart assembly.
    \item $B_{eq}$ is the equivalent linear viscous damping coefficient, capturing the lumped dissipative losses across the track, bearings, and gears.
    \item $F_c$ is the driving linear force delivered to the rack by the DC motor pinion.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Actuator Dynamics and Electromechanical Coupling}
The driving force $F_c$ originates from the electrical domain, where the input armature voltage $V_m$ generates an electromagnetic torque. Accounting for back-EMF and armature resistance, the force is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F_c &= \frac{\eta_g  K_g k_t}{R_m r_{mp}} \left( \eta_m \cdot v_m - \frac{K_g k_m \cdot \dot{x}_c}{r_{mp}} \right)\\
        &= \alpha \cdot v_m - B_{emf} \cdot \dot{x}_c
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Expanding this expression allows the electrical and mechanical dependencies to be cleanly decoupled into a voltage-proportional force gain and an intrinsic electrical damping term, namely $\alpha$ and $B_{emf}$.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
Substituting the actuator force expression into the mechanical force balance yields the explicit differential equation mapping the control input voltage $V_m(t)$ directly to the cart spatial acceleration $\ddot{x}_c(t)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha \cdot v_m(t) - B_{emf}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{eq,c}\cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation*}
Grouping the velocity-dependent terms highlights the total open-loop damping present within the system:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_eom}
    \alpha \cdot v(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{tot}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t)
\end{equation}
Where $B_{tot} = B_{eq} + B_{emf}$ represents the combined effective damping coefficient, encapsulating both mechanical friction losses and back-EMF braking effects. This consolidated linear model serves as the analytical foundation for subsequent system identification and control loop synthesis.

\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Euler-Lagrange Equation}
To accurately model the multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) coupled system, the analytical derivation is performed using the energy-based Euler-Lagrange framework. We select the generalized coordinates vector as:
\begin{equation}
    q = \begin{bmatrix}
        x_c \\ \theta
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
First, considering the baseline moment of inertia of the seesaw assembly about its physical pivot point, we define $J_{pivot}$ as:
\begin{equation*}
    J_{pivot} = J_{SW} + M_C \cdot D_C^2
\end{equation*}
The total kinetic energy $T$ of the coupled system accounts for the linear translation of the cart, the pure rotation of the seesaw base, and the non-linear velocity coupling terms arising from the moving reference frame:
\begin{equation}
	T = \frac{1}{2}M_e \cdot \dot{x}_c^2 + \frac{1}{2}(J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2)\cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{1}{2} M_{tot} \cdot x_c^2 \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}
\end{equation}
Where $M_{tot} = M_c + M_w$ represents the total combined active mass of the translating assembly.\\
The system potential energy $V$ is defined relative to the horizontal datum plane, capturing the height variations of both the translating cart mass along the inclined rail and the shift in the center of gravity of the rotating seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	V = g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot sin(\theta) + g( M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C ) \cdot cos(\theta)
\end{equation}
Using these energy definitions, the system Lagrangian is established as $L = T - V$. The explicit equations of motion are generated by applying the Euler-Lagrange operator to each generalized coordinate:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = Q_i
\end{equation}
Where $Q_i$ represents the vector of generalized non-conservative forces and dissipative torques acting on the system. For the translational and rotational coordinates respectively, the formulation expands to:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{x}_c} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_c}       & = - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c +  \alpha \cdot v_m\\
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{\theta}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} & = - B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta}
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
By executing the partial differentiations explicitly, this system yields a set of coupled, non-linear second-order differential equations that govern the global state-space profile of the cart-seesaw plant. These equations serve as the analytical foundation for subsequent linearization, system identification, and model-based controller synthesis.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
By explicitly evaluating the Euler-Lagrange equations, we obtain the coupled, non-linear multi-input multi-output (MIMO) dynamic equations for the complete cart-seesaw system. The resulting system models the full electromechanical translation of the cart alongside the rotational dynamics of the supporting seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:seesaw_eom}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} - M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + g M_{tot} \cdot sin(\theta)                 & =   \alpha_f \cdot v_m \\
		M_{tot} \cdot \ddot{\theta} \cdot x_c^2 + 2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot cos(\theta) \cdot x_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c & +                            \\
		(J_{sw} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{sw}D_C) \cdot sin(\theta)                             & = 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_m(t)$ is the control input voltage applied to the linear base unit motor, standardizing the actuator parameters derived previously.
    \item The centripetal acceleration component $M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2$ and the Coriolis coupling term $2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}$ represent the highly non-linear dynamic forces acting across the moving reference frames.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Linearization}
Because these coupled non-linear equations are unsuited for traditional linear control framework designs, the system must be linearized around its unstable equilibrium point. We define the nominal operating equilibrium where the cart rests at the center of the track and the seesaw is perfectly horizontal:
\begin{equation*}
    (x_{c,0}, \theta_0) = (0,0)
\end{equation*}
To perform a small-signal linearization around this equilibrium state, the following assumptions are enforced:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Small angles ($\theta \simeq 0$) : $sin(\theta) = \theta$ and $cos(\theta) = 1$
	\item Small displacement ($x_c \simeq 0$)
	\item Ignoring high-order velocity terms : $\dot{\theta}^2, x_c^2, \dot{x}_c\dot{\theta} \simeq 0$
\end{enumerate}
Applying these assumptions eliminates the non-linear coupling constraints and simplifies the framework into a set of linearly coupled differential equations. This linearized model describes the small-signal perturbations of the system near the origin:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \\
            - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot \theta  
            & =  \alpha \cdot v_m\\
		- M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c + g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \\
            + (J_{sw} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{sw}D_C) \cdot \theta 
            &= 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
This finalized linear differential model forms the definitive analytical baseline for executing transfer function derivations, state-space mapping, and closed-loop controller synthesis throughout the remainder of this report.

\subsection{Non-linearities mitigation}
During experimental validation of the IP02, discrepancies emerged between the idealized linear models and the physical hardware response. These discrepancies are primarily driven by localized, hard non-linearities—specifically static friction, Coulomb friction, and actuator deadzones inherent to the rack-and-pinion and planetary gearbox assembly.\\
Left uncompensated, these non-linearities introduce steady-state tracking errors, limit cycles, and severe phase distortions that degrade linear controller performance. To restore the validity of the linear assumptions, a data-driven system identification routine was executed to map these non-linear profiles, followed by the implementation of a software-based feedforward inverse-deadband compensation scheme.\\

\end{figure}
The underlying mathematical model for this non-symmetric voltage deadzone is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{eff}} = f(V_{\text{cmd}}) = 
\begin{cases} 
    V_{\text{cmd}} + u_{d,\text{pos}} & \text{if } V_{\text{cmd}} > \epsilon \\
    0 & \text{if } |V_{\text{cmd}}| \le \epsilon \\ 
    V_{\text{cmd}} - u_{d,\text{neg}} & \text{if } V_{\text{cmd}} < -\epsilon \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\epsilon$ is the deadzone threshold, to avoid chattering between positive and negative motion.
    \item $V_{\text{cmd}}$ is the raw voltage command generated by the control architecture.
    \item $V_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective, torque-producing voltage delivered to the motor armature after overcoming friction.
    \item $u_{d,\text{pos}}$ and $u_{d,\text{neg}}$ represent the distinct positive and negative voltage thresholds required to break static friction.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Characterization via Dynamic Modular Signaling}
To cleanly decouple velocity-dependent Coulomb friction from zero-velocity stiction, the system was excited using a structured modular input signal designed to sweep through multiple distinct dynamic phases. This approach guarantees that the cart transitions smoothly between static equilibrium, rapid acceleration profiles, and steady-state velocity regimes, exposing the underlying non-symmetric nature of the voltage deadzone.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Deadzone Extraction}
The script \texttt{seesaw\_nonlinear\_model.m} processes the raw hardware dataset gathered via QUARC to isolate these tresholds. The identification pipeline executes across the following key stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Phase Velocity Estimation}: To circumvent the severe phase-lag and noise amplification associated with raw numerical differentiation, the cart position coordinate ($x_c$) is first pre-processed using a forward-backward digital filter (2nd-order \textit{Butterworth} via texttt{filtfilt}). The cutoff frequency is dynamically scaled relative to the system's structural time constant:
    \begin{equation*}
        \omega _{c,butter} = 2 \cdot \frac{B_{tot}}{M_e}
    \end{equation*}
    \item \textbf{Sustained-Motion Masking}: Active velocity states are isolated from measurement noise and micro-oscillations by enforcing a strict kinematic threshold constraint:
    \begin{equation*}
        |\dot{x}_c| > v_{tresh} = 0.002 [m/s]
    \end{equation*}
    A logical persistence routine ensures that only prolonged, continuous-motion vectors are used for parameter estimation, completely stripping out transient encoder ticks.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Parameter Mapping}: The algorithm splits the valid motion index vectors into directional groups to capture hardware asymmetry. By assessing the minimum absolute input voltage applied during these validated windows, the precise directional deadzone boundaries are computed:
    \begin{align*}
        u_{d,\text{pos}} &= \min \left( |V_{\text{diff}}| \right) &\forall \ \{k \ \in \ \mathbb{Z}^+ \ | \ \dot{x}_c(k) > v_{\text{thresh}} \ \land \ V_{\text{diff}}(k) > 0\}\\
        u_{d,\text{neg}} &= \min \left( |V_{\text{diff}}| \right) &\forall \ \{k \ \in \ \mathbb{Z}^+ \ | \ \dot{x}_c(k) < -v_{\text{thresh}} \ \land \ V_{\text{diff}}(k) < 0\}
    \end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Software-Based Inverse-Deadband Compensation}
Using the extracted parameters stored in \texttt{param\_nonlinear.mat}, an online feedforward compensation block was placed directly upstream of the Digital-to-Analog Converters (DAC). This software block applies an algebraic inverse mapping function to the controller's synthesized voltage output ($V_{ctrl}$) before routing the signal to the power amplifier:
\begin{equation*}
    V_{\text{DAC}} = \begin{cases} V_{\text{ctrl}} + u_{d,\text{pos}} & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} > \epsilon \\ 0 & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} = 0 \\ V_{\text{ctrl}} - u_{d,\text{neg}} & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} < \epsilon \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
By injecting this step-discontinuity at the zero-crossing point, the controller effectively "pre-shifts" the control effort, neutralizing the actuator deadband. This mathematical cancellation linearizes the input-to-state tracking response, ensuring that the cart's physical translation mirrors the synthesized linear state-space expectations during highly dynamic, bidirectional maneuvers.

\section{Dynamic Modeling \& System Identification}
\subsection{Cart Analytical Modeling: Transfer Function Definition}
Based on the equation of motion described at Eq.~\ref{eq:cart_eom}, we define the transfer function of the system using the Laplace Transform (keeping the assumption of zero initial condition).
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:cart_eom_laplace}
	\alpha \cdot V_m(s) = [M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s]\cdot X_c(s)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_tf}
    G_x(s) = \frac{X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha}{M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Cart Parameter Estimation \& Open-Loop Time Validation} \label{sec:cart_model_tuning}
The section~\ref{sec:cart_model_tuning} is aimed at finding values that match better our real system in order to have a model precise-enough. In this section, most of the values obtained from Tab.~\ref{table:datasheet} are assumed to be true. The focus will be put on the viscous damping coefficient $B_{eq,w}$ and the planetary gearbox efficiency $\eta_g$.\\
Other parameters have been considered like the motor resistance $R_m$ or the motor efficiency $\eta_m$ but their electromagnetic properties are more stable than the mechanical gearbox that can be subject to dust, wear, etc.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
Since the position transfer function has a pure integrator, it is considered to have theoretically infinite static gain. \\
Therefore, it is the velocity transfer function that will be preferred for this part: 
\begin{equation}
    G_v(s) = \frac{\alpha}{M_e \cdot s + B_{tot}}
\end{equation}
This plant is stable, with an unique pole in $p_{cart} = -\frac{B_{tot}}{M_e}$ and its characteristics are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item static gain $K_{DC} = \frac{\alpha(\eta_g)}{B_{tot}(\eta_g,B_{eq,w})}$
    \item time constant $\tau = \frac{M_e}{B_{tot}(\eta_g,B_{eq,w})}$
\end{itemize}
The experiment simply goes as follow: input a step voltage and record the position. To clear the sensor noise while avoiding any phase lag, a low-pass filter is applied in post-processing to allow a clean differentiation from position to velocity. From the recorded data, the reals static gain and time constant can be computed. Matlab implements natively optimization algorithm such as fminsearch that permits to find the best $\eta_g$ and $B_{eq,w}$ that will fit the measurements.\\
Second hand tuning can be performed if the optimization results are not convincing.
\subsection{Cart Open-Loop Frequency Validation}
\subsubsection{Objective}
To design a stable and robust cascade controller for the seesaw control, the inner-loop cart position dynamics must be accurately modeled. This section details the empirical frequency-domain validation of the theoretical linear transfer function \( G_x(s) \), mapping the commanded voltage \( V_{cmd} \) to the cart position \( x_c \).

\subsubsection{Methodology: Schroeder-Phased Multi-Sine Injection}
A frequency sweep was conducted using a Schroeder-phased multi-sine signal rather than a traditional chirp or stepped-sine approach. This methodology provides several distinct advantages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hardware Protection:} Schroeder phasing minimizes the crest factor of the composite signal. This maximizes the energy injected at each target frequency without exceeding the absolute peak voltage limits (\( V_{max} \)) or generating excessive mechanical shear forces at the rack and pinion.
    \item \textbf{Timescale Efficiency:} It allows for the simultaneous injection of \( N \) logarithmically spaced frequencies, capturing the entire target spectrum in a single, continuous test window.
\end{enumerate}

Prior to deployment, a safety script was utilized to simulate the multi-sine signal through the theoretical models, confirming that maximum dynamic forces and absolute track displacements remained within safe operational bounds.

\subsubsection{Digital Signal Processing and Spectral Extraction}
Extracting the Frequency Response Function (FRF) from the raw hardware telemetry requires robust DSP techniques to mitigate noise and spectral leakage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transient Trimming:} The initial seconds of the data were discarded to ensure the cart had settled into a steady-state limit cycle.
    \item \textbf{Detrending and Windowing:} The steady-state data was detrended to remove slow positional drift and multiplied by a Hann window. Because the logarithmically generated frequencies are incommensurate (non-integer multiples), the Hann window is critical to prevent spectral leakage from the high-amplitude, low-frequency position data into the low-amplitude, high-frequency data.
    \item \textbf{Targeted Spectral Estimation:} Instead of a standard Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the empirical magnitude and phase were extracted using Welch's method (\texttt{tfestimate} in MATLAB), specifically evaluated only at the exact injected frequencies. This effectively acts as a lock-in amplifier, completely ignoring unmodeled broadband noise and isolated gear chatter.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{blue}{Gemini: Open-Loop Frequency Validation of the Cart Position Dynamics}}

\subsubsection{Objective}
To design a stable and robust cascade controller for the seesaw control, the inner-loop cart position dynamics must be accurately modeled. This section details the empirical frequency-domain validation of the theoretical linear transfer function \( G_x(s) \), mapping the commanded voltage \( V_{cmd} \) to the cart position \( x_c \).

\subsubsection{Methodology: Schroeder-Phased Multi-Sine Injection}
A frequency sweep was conducted using a Schroeder-phased multi-sine signal rather than a traditional chirp or stepped-sine approach. This methodology provides several distinct advantages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hardware Protection:} Schroeder phasing minimizes the crest factor of the composite signal. This maximizes the energy injected at each target frequency without exceeding the absolute peak voltage limits (\( V_{max} \)) or generating excessive mechanical shear forces at the rack and pinion.
    \item \textbf{Timescale Efficiency:} It allows for the simultaneous injection of \( N \) logarithmically spaced frequencies, capturing the entire target spectrum in a single, continuous test window.
\end{enumerate}

Prior to deployment, a safety script was utilized to simulate the multi-sine signal through the theoretical models, confirming that maximum dynamic forces and absolute track displacements remained within safe operational bounds.

\subsubsection{Digital Signal Processing and Spectral Extraction}
Extracting the Frequency Response Function (FRF) from the raw hardware telemetry requires robust DSP techniques to mitigate noise and spectral leakage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transient Trimming:} The initial seconds of the data were discarded to ensure the cart had settled into a steady-state limit cycle.
    \item \textbf{Detrending and Windowing:} The steady-state data was detrended to remove slow positional drift and multiplied by a Hann window. Because the logarithmically generated frequencies are incommensurate (non-integer multiples), the Hann window is critical to prevent spectral leakage from the high-amplitude, low-frequency position data into the low-amplitude, high-frequency data.
    \item \textbf{Targeted Spectral Estimation:} Instead of a standard Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the empirical magnitude and phase were extracted using Welch's method (\texttt{tfestimate} in MATLAB), specifically evaluated only at the exact injected frequencies. This effectively acts as a lock-in amplifier, completely ignoring unmodeled broadband noise and isolated gear chatter.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results and Analysis}
The empirical FRF data was overlaid onto the theoretical continuous-time Bode plot of \( G_x(s) \). The frequency response can be divided into three operational regions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low-Frequency Band ($<$ \SI{1}{\radian\per\second}) - The Nonlinear Penalty:}\\
    In the lowest frequencies, the empirical phase exhibits a noticeable lead artifact. This is not a physical acceleration, but rather the describing function signature of the friction compensator. Because the low-frequency sine waves are shallow, the signal spends significant time inside the compensator's zero-voltage deadband (\( \epsilon \)), causing a momentary stick-slip effect that the DSP algorithm interprets as phase distortion. Because the outer-loop target bandwidth (\( \omega_{c,outer} \approx \SI{4.5}{\radian\per\second} \)) is significantly higher than this region, this artifact is easily rejected by the integral action of the controller.

    \item \textbf{Mid-Frequency Band (\SI{1}{\radian\per\second} to \SI{10}{\radian\per\second}) - The Target Operation Zone:}\\
    Within this critical band, the empirical data and the theoretical \( G_x(s) \) model correlate near-perfectly in both magnitude and phase. This validates the mathematical modeling of the equivalent mass (\( M_e \)) and viscous damping (\( B_{eq} \)), confirming that the model is highly accurate in the exact region where the non-minimum phase inverted pendulum loop will operate.

    \item \textbf{High-Frequency Band ($>$ \SI{10}{\radian\per\second}) - Unmodeled Time Delay:}\\
    While the magnitude continues to track the theoretical roll-off, the empirical phase diverges, dropping steadily below the theoretical curve (reaching approximately \(-150^\circ\) at \SI{30}{\radian\per\second}). This divergence is a classic symptom of unmodeled digital and hardware time delay (\( e^{-s T_d} \)), comprised of the Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) penalty from the \SI{2}{\milli\second} sample rate, USB communication latency, and potential hidden low-pass filtering on the encoder derivatives.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cart Modeling Conclusion}
The open-loop cart dynamics \( G_x(s) \) are successfully validated for control design. The linear model is highly accurate in the primary operational bandwidth. However, when designing the inner-loop cascade controller (target bandwidth \( \approx \SI{22.5}{\radian\per\second} \)), the physical phase droop observed in the high-frequency band dictates that the Lead compensator must provide an additional \( 20^\circ \) to \( 30^\circ \) of phase margin beyond what the theoretical continuous model suggests.

\subsection{Transfer Function Definition}

Based on the equation of motion described at Eq.~\ref{eq:cart_eom}, we define the transfer function of the system using the Laplace Transform (assuming zero initial conditions).
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:cart_eom_laplace}
	\alpha \cdot V_m(s) = [M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s]\cdot X_c(s)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_tf}
    G_x(s) = \frac{X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha}{M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Parameters Tuning} \label{sec:tuning}
The section~\ref{sec:tuning} is aimed at finding values that match better our real system in order to have a model precise-enough. In this section, most of the values obtained from Tab.~\ref{table:datasheet} are assumed to be true. The focus will be put on the viscous damping coefficient $B_{eq,w}$ and the planetary gearbox efficiency $\eta_g$.\\
Other parameters have been considered like the motor resistance $R_m$ or the motor efficiency $\eta_m$ but their electromagnetic properties are more stable than the mechanical gearbox that can be subject to dust, wear, etc.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
Since the position transfer function has a pure integrator, it is considered to have theoretically infinite static gain. \\
Therefore, it is the velocity transfer function that will be preferred for this part: 
\begin{equation}
    G_v(s) = \frac{\alpha}{M_e \cdot s + B_{tot}}
\end{equation}
This plant is stable, with an unique pole in $p_{cart} = -\frac{B_{tot}}{M_e}$ and its characteristics are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item static gain $K_{DC} = \frac{\alpha(\eta_g)}{B_{tot}(\eta_g,B_{eq,w})}$
    \item time constant $\tau = \frac{M_e}{B_{tot}(\eta_g,B_{eq,w})}$
\end{itemize}
The experiment simply goes as follow: input a step voltage and record the position. To clear the sensor noise while avoiding any phase lag, a low-pass filter is applied in post-processing to allow a clean differentiation from position to velocity. From the recorded data, the reals static gain and time constant can be computed. Matlab implements natively optimization algorithm such as fminsearch that permits to find the best $\eta_g$ and $B_{eq,w}$ that will fit the measurements.\\
Second hand tuning can be performed if the optimization results are not convincing.

\subsection{Control of the position: simulation}\label{sec:CartPosControl}
For the cart position control, a 2-DOF architecture is implemented. This approach separates the objectives of command tracking (handled by feedforward) and disturbance rejection/stability (handled by feedback).

\subsubsection{Feedforward Control $C_{FF}(s)$}
This controller is designed to provide the necessary control effort for the desired trajectory, reducing the stress of the feedback loop. The feedforward compensator aims at mimicking the inverse dynamic, $C_{FF}(s) = G_x^{-1}(s)$. However this controller would not be proper, therefore band-limited derivative filters are used to define the inverse dynamic:
\begin{equation*}
    C_{FF}(s) = K_a \cdot s^2 \cdot H^2_{bessel}(s) + K_v \cdot s \cdot H_{bessel}(s)
\end{equation*}
Where
\begin{align*}
    K_a &= \frac{M_e}{\alpha}, & K_v &= \frac{B_{tot}}{\alpha}
\end{align*}
The Bessel filters are second-order filter to prevent high-frequency noises, with a cutoff frequency targeted to be 2 to 10 times faster than the plant pole $p_{cart}$.
\begin{equation*}
    w_{c,bessel} = 7 \cdot p_{cart}
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Feedback Control $C_x(s)$}
The feedback controller $C_{FB}$ is designed in the frequency domain to ensure stability and robust disturbance rejection.\\
The classic choice is a PID Controller, but with a N-filter to make it strictly proper. The plant being stable, this tuning is more performance oriented.\\
The final objective is already know : use this controller as an inner-loop for cascade architecture. This creates the tuning factor, the outside plant is known to have an unstable pole around $+2.75 [rad/s]$.\\ This will force the outer-loop to have a bandwidth 2 to 5 times faster than the unstable pole, and so on the inner-loop bandwidth should be 3 to 10 times faster than the outer-loop one. The obtained range is $\omega _b \in (16.5;137.5) [rad/s]$. \\
Another requirement is the phase margin, ideally overshoot must be prevented so an high phase margin is preferred : $\phi _ {pm} \in (70;90)[^\circ]$.\\
Also, the N-filter where $N$ is fixed at $N = \omega_{c,bessel}$ will introduce phase lag of:
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta \phi = - arctan\left(\frac{\omega_b}{N}\right)
\end{equation*}
to keep in mind during the coefficient tuning.\\
Theoretically, in continuous time $G_x(s)$ has infinite gain margin, therefore it is not a relevant criterion to tune the PID parameters.\\
\begin{equation*}
    C_x(s) = K_c \frac{(s + z_1)(s + z_2)}{s(s + N)}
\end{equation*}
Where $z_1$ is placed at $\omega_b/10$ to provide low-frequency integral action, while $z_2$ is analytically calculated to satisfy the required Phase Margin at the crossover frequency.\\
The final simulated PID coefficients are:
\begin{align*}
    K_p &= 149.3098 [V/m] &
    K_i &= 217.7877 [V/(m \cdot s)] &
    K_d &= 19.5673 [V \cdot s/m]
\end{align*}
These coefficient might seem high, but the efficient work of $C_{FF}(s)$ allows the position error to stay low at all time

\subsection{Parameter Tuning}
Most of the datasheet's parameters can be trusted, but the dynamics depends a lot on an accurate center of gravity.\\
Therefore we perform a \emph{Quasi-Static Lift-up Test} to verify $D_C$. The procedure involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immobilizing the Cart: The cart is fixed at a known position $x_{min} \simeq -\frac{T_c}{2} = -0.407[m]$ and angle $\theta_{min} \simeq e_{range} = 11.5[^\circ]$.
    \item Force Application: A constant voltage is applied to the cart for it to reach velocity steady-state (i.e. $\ddot{x}_c = 0$).
    \item Lift-up Measurement: At the exact time the seesaw lifts-up, measure $x_{lift}$ and compute the distance from CoG from Eq.~\ref{eq:lift-up}.
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lift-up}
    D_C = \frac{M_e \cdot x_{lift} - M_e D_T \cdot \theta_{min}}{M_{sw}\cdot \theta_{min}}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Frequency-Based control}\label{subsec:freq_based_control}
Observing $G_\theta(s)$, there is two problems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The plant is a proper, but not strictly proper, transfer function. Meaning that the magnitude does not attenuate at higher frequency.
    \item It has both an unstable pole and an unstable zero, making it a \emph{Non-Minimum Phase} System.
\end{enumerate}
Separately the two instabilities, RHP pole and RHP zero, are stabilizable under the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item RHP pole in $p_0$ imposes a lower bound on the bandwidth (around $\omega_b > 2\cdot p_0$) because the controller must be fast enough to catch the system before it drifts away.
	\item RHP zero in $z_0$ imposes an upper bound on the bandwidth (around $\omega_b < \frac{z_0}{2}$) because $z_0$ makes the system "go in the wrong direction at first" (= undershoot)
\end{itemize}
Adapting it back to our unstable plant, 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The unstable pole in $p_0 = +2.7754 [rad/s]$ impose a minimal bandwidth of $\omega_b > 5.5508 [rad/s]$.
    \item The unstable zero in $z_0 = +8.8589[rad/s]$ imposes a maximal bandwidth of $\omega_b < 4.4295 [rad/s]$.
\end{itemize}
These limit values being theoretical, an attempt to control such system will still be performed.\\
Similarly to the previous setup, we try to generate controllers such that the global open-loop and closed-loop system are meeting stability and performance criterion. The standard approach is to use a cascade architecture :
\begin{itemize}
	\item A fast inner loop, already tuned at the sec.~\ref{sec:CartPosControl}, that controls the position $X_c(s)$ based on the motor action.
	\item A slower outer loop, that controls the angle $\Theta(s)$ and its impact on the cart position.
\end{itemize}
This means that we will consider for this step:
\begin{align*}
	T_{in}(s) & = \frac{G_x(s) \cdot (C_{FF}(s) + C_x(s))}{1 + G_x(s) \cdot C_x(s)} \\
    P_{out}(s) &= T_{in}(s) \cdot G_\theta (s) \\
    %C_\theta(s) &= K_{p,out} + \frac{K_{i,out}}{s} + K_{d,out} \cdot \frac{N_{out}\cdot s}{s + N_{out}}\\
    %L_\theta (s) & = C_\theta(s) \cdot P_{out}(s)\\
     %T_\theta(s) & = \frac{L_\theta (s)}{1 + L_\theta (s)}
\end{align*}

\subsection{LQR \textcolor{red}{Do not modify this section}}
LQR is a full-state feedback optimization algorithm.
For the seesaw it translates to : instead of measuring the angle $\theta$ that we want to control, LQR assume to know the current state of everything in the system. It defines the feedback law as
\begin{align}\label{eq:LQR_FB_law_and_states}
	u & = -K \cdot \mathbf{x}, & \mathbf{x} & = \begin{bmatrix}
		                                            x_c \\ \theta \\ \dot{x_c} \\ \dot{\theta}
	                                            \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
LQR finds this optimal controller by minimizing a cost function $J$ based on Riccati's equation, see Appendix~\ref{app:ARE}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LQR_cost}
	J = \int _0 ^\infty (\mathbf{x}^\top \cdot Q \cdot \mathbf{x} +  \mathbf{u}^\top \cdot R \cdot \mathbf{u})dt
\end{equation}
The tuning parameters of LQR are the weight matrices Q and R, finding the balance between them defines how aggressive and "willing to spend more effort" the system will be.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Q} : The \emph{State Error} matrix (or Performance Penalty matrix) emphasize what state are important to control. A higher value $q_i$ means a more controlled state $i$, and a lower value means that any value can be accepted.
	      \begin{equation}
		      \mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix}
			      q_1 & 0   & 0   & 0   \\
			      0   & q_2 & 0   & 0   \\
			      0   & 0   & q_3 & 0   \\
			      0   & 0   & 0   & q_4 \\
		      \end{bmatrix}
		      \geq 0
	      \end{equation}
	\item \textbf{R} : The \emph{Input Error} matrix (or Effort Penalty matrix) penalizes the input states. As we only have one input for the seesaw, it is a scalar. A higher R means that the voltage must be lowered at all time, while a lower R tells the algorithm to use more power.
	      \begin{equation}
		      \mathbf{R} = r_1
	      \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
This solution gain $K$ can be interpreted, in standard algebra as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		V_{DC} & = -K\cdot \mathbf{x}                                         \\
		       & = -(k_1 x_c + k_2 \theta + k_3 \dot{x}_c + k_4 \dot{\theta})
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This can easily be compared with what we were trying to do with the frequency-based technique, sec.~\ref{subsec:freq_based_control}, but this time instead of "guessing" how the cascade \emph{PD Controllers} should interact, the Riccati's Equation guarantees their balance.
\begin{align}
	V_{DC} = \underbrace{(-k_1 x_c - k_3 \dot{x}_c)}_{\text{Cart PD Controller}} + \underbrace{(-k_2 \theta - k_4 \dot{\theta})}_{\text{Angle PD Controller}}
\end{align}

\subsection{LQR Controller Design}

The Seesaw controller is designed from the linearized state-space model of the cart--seesaw system around the horizontal equilibrium. The state vector is defined as
\[
	x =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta}
	\end{bmatrix}^T
\]
where $x_c$ is the cart displacement and $\theta$ is the seesaw angle. The linearized dynamics are written in the standard form
\[
	\dot{x} = Ax + Bu.
\]

Two LQR-based controllers were considered. The first one is a standard 4-state feedback controller, with control law
\[
	u = -K_4 x,
	\qquad
	K_4 = \mathrm{lqr}(A,B,Q_4,R_4).
\]

The feedback gain is obtained by minimizing the quadratic cost function
\[
	J_4 = \int_0^{\infty} \left( x^T Q_4 x + u^T R_4 u \right)\, dt
\]
where $Q_4 \succeq 0$ penalizes the state deviation and $R_4 \succ 0$ penalizes the control effort. In the initial design, the weighting matrices were selected as
\[
	Q_4 = I_4,
	\qquad
	R_4 = 1.
\]

The controllability analysis shows that the 4-state model is fully controllable, with
\[
	\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{C}_4 = 4.
\]
Its open-loop poles include one unstable positive real pole, confirming that the horizontal equilibrium is open-loop unstable. After applying LQR feedback, the closed-loop poles move to the left half-plane, so the system becomes asymptotically stable. The resulting gain matrix is
\[
	K_4 =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		33.7867 & -28.7640 & 6.8134 & -10.6475
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]

This controller provides a relatively fast transient response and limited cart displacement, but it does not include integral action. Therefore, its ability to reject residual steady-state error caused by model mismatch or constant disturbances is inherently limited.

To improve regulation, an augmented 5-state LQR design was also introduced. An additional integral state is defined as
\[
	\xi = \int \theta(t)\,dt,
	\qquad
	\dot{\xi} = \theta,
\]
so that the augmented state vector becomes
\[
	\eta =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta} & \xi
	\end{bmatrix}^T.
\]

The corresponding augmented dynamics are written as
\[
	\dot{\eta} = A_5 \eta + B_5 u
\]
and the control law becomes
\[
	u = -K_5 \eta,
	\qquad
	K_5 = \mathrm{lqr}(A_5,B_5,Q_5,R_5).
\]

The augmented gain is obtained by minimizing the cost function
\[
	J_5 = \int_0^{\infty} \left( \eta^T Q_5 \eta + u^T R_5 u \right)\, dt.
\]

For this controller, the baseline weighting matrices were chosen as
\[
	Q_5 = \mathrm{diag}(1000,\;5000,\;0,\;0,\;2000),
	\qquad
	R_5 = 0.5.
\]

The augmented system is also fully controllable, with
\[
	\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{C}_5 = 5.
\]
As expected, the open-loop augmented model contains an additional pole at the origin due to the integral state. After feedback is applied, all closed-loop poles lie in the stable region, confirming that the augmented controller also stabilizes the system. The corresponding gain vector is
\[
	K_5 =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		125.4935 & -214.7644 & 21.9155 & -68.1839 & -63.2456
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Compared with the 4-gain design, the augmented controller introduces integral action and is therefore better suited to reducing residual regulation error in the presence of non-ideal effects.

The role of the weighting matrices follows the standard LQR interpretation. Increasing the weights associated with $x_c$ and $\theta$ gives higher priority to cart-position and angle regulation, respectively. Increasing the weight on the integral state $\xi$ strengthens the long-term correction of angular deviation and generally improves regulation speed, although it may also make the controller more aggressive. On the other hand, increasing $R$ penalizes the control input more heavily, resulting in a more conservative controller with smoother actuation but slower closed-loop dynamics.

The controllers were implemented and tested in Simulink under the same initial disturbance. In the nominal case, the 4-gain controller produced a faster response and a smaller maximum cart displacement, with peak values approximately
\[
	|x_c|_{\max} \approx 0.055~\text{m},
	\qquad
	|\theta|_{\max} \approx 0.073~\text{rad},
	\qquad
	t_s \approx 3.16~\text{s}.
\]
The 5-gain controller reduced the angular peak to about
\[
	|\theta|_{\max} \approx 0.056~\text{rad},
\]
but required a larger cart motion and a longer settling time, namely
\[
	|x_c|_{\max} \approx 0.073~\text{m},
	\qquad
	t_s \approx 5.31~\text{s}.
\]
Therefore, the 4-gain controller can be regarded as the more responsive design, whereas the 5-gain controller offers better angular regulation and a stronger theoretical capability to reject residual steady-state error.

A further tuning study was performed on the augmented controller. Increasing $R$ made the controller more conservative by reducing cart excursion and slowing down the response. Increasing $Q_{11}$ and $Q_{33}$ reduced cart motion, while increasing $Q_{55}$, the weight associated with the integral state, significantly accelerated regulation but also made the control action more aggressive.

Based on this study, two practical parameter sets were retained. The \emph{balanced} preset uses
\[
	Q_5 = \mathrm{diag}(1000,\;5000,\;100,\;0,\;2000),
	\qquad
	R_5 = 0.5
\]
with corresponding gain
\[
	K_5 =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		162.3858 & -241.3832 & 30.0771 & -80.6462 & -63.2456
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This case provides a good compromise between angle regulation, settling time, and cart displacement.

The \emph{conservative} preset uses
\[
	Q_5 = \mathrm{diag}(1000,\;5000,\;100,\;0,\;2000),
	\qquad
	R_5 = 2
\]
with corresponding gain
\[
	K_5 =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		101.4322 & -136.8471 & 18.9892 & -46.9145 & -31.6228
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This case is less aggressive, since it slightly reduces cart motion and weakens the control action, at the price of a slower response.

Among the tested options, the balanced design is selected as the preferred final choice, while the conservative design remains a useful alternative when smaller cart excursion and softer actuation are preferred.

Meanwhile, it should be noted that the actuator voltage is limited to $\pm 6~\text{V}$, and this saturation is reached in several simulations. As a result, the implemented response is not the ideal unconstrained LQR solution, but rather the behavior of an LQR controller operating under input saturation. This is especially relevant for the augmented controller, since integral action may lead to windup when the actuator is saturated. For this reason, future refinements should include a more detailed analysis of saturation duration, control effort, and possibly the introduction of an anti-windup strategy.

\subsection{LQI Controller Design}

To further improve regulation and tracking performance, an LQI (\textit{Linear Quadratic Integral}) controller was also considered. The LQI approach extends the standard LQR design by augmenting the system with an integral state associated with the output error. In this way, the controller not only stabilizes the system, but also reduces or eliminates steady-state error in the presence of constant disturbances, modeling inaccuracies, or nonzero reference commands.

For the Seesaw system, the controlled output is chosen as the angular position $\theta$. Defining the regulation error as
\[
	e(t)=r(t)-\theta(t),
\]
where $r(t)$ is the reference angle, the integral state is introduced as
\[
	\xi(t)=\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]
For the regulation problem considered in this work, the reference is $r(t)=0$, so the error reduces to
\[
	e(t)=-\theta(t),
\]
and therefore
\[
	\dot{\xi}(t)=-\theta(t).
\]

Using the original state vector
\[
	x=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta}
	\end{bmatrix}^T,
\]
the augmented state vector becomes
\[
	x_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x \\
		\xi
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta} & \xi
	\end{bmatrix}^T.
\]

The augmented system can then be written as
\[
	\dot{x}_a=A_a x_a+B_a u+B_r r
\]
with
\[
	A_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		A         & 0 \\
		-C_\theta & 0
	\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	B_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		B \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	B_r=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		1
	\end{bmatrix},
\]
where
\[
	C_\theta=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & 1 & 0 & 0
	\end{bmatrix}
\]
selects the angular state.

The LQI gain is obtained by minimizing the quadratic cost function
\[
	J_{\mathrm{LQI}}
	=
	\int_0^\infty
	\left(
	x_a^T Q_a x_a + u^T R u
	\right)\,dt
\]
where $Q_a \succeq 0$ weights both the physical states and the integral state, while $R \succ 0$ penalizes the control effort. The optimal control law is
\[
	u=-K_a x_a
	=
	-\begin{bmatrix}
		K_x & K_i
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x \\
		\xi
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]

In this formulation, the matrix $K_x$ acts on the original plant states, while the scalar gain $K_i$ acts on the integral state. The integral action continuously accumulates the angle error and reinforces the control input until the steady-state deviation is removed. For this reason, the LQI controller is theoretically more effective than a standard LQR controller whenever constant disturbances, parameter uncertainty, or reference changes are present.

In the present Seesaw application, the augmented controller previously designed with five gains can be interpreted as an LQI regulator for the angular position. The selected weighting matrix
\[
	Q_a=\mathrm{diag}(1000,\;5000,\;0,\;0,\;2000),
	\qquad
	R=0.5
\]
assigns strong importance to the angular displacement and to the integral state, thereby improving long-term regulation of the horizontal equilibrium. The resulting gain vector is
\[
	K_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		125.4935 & -214.7644 & 21.9155 & -68.1839 & -63.2456
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Compared with the standard 4-gain LQR controller, the LQI controller produces a slower transient response and a larger cart displacement, but it reduces the angular peak and provides a stronger theoretical guarantee of zero steady-state error for constant disturbances or modeling mismatch. This makes the LQI structure particularly suitable when accurate long-term balancing around the horizontal position is more important than achieving the fastest possible transient.

As in the LQR case, the tuning of the LQI controller is governed by the choice of the weighting matrices. Increasing the weight associated with the integral state generally improves steady-state regulation and speeds up error correction, but it also makes the control action more aggressive. Increasing $R$ leads instead to a more conservative controller with smaller input effort and slower convergence.

Finally, it should be emphasized that the actuator voltage is limited to $\pm 6~\mathrm{V}$. Under saturation, the integral state may continue to accumulate error even when the actuator can no longer provide additional effort. This phenomenon, known as integral windup, may degrade performance and should be addressed in a practical implementation through an anti-windup strategy. Nevertheless, the simulation results confirm that the LQI controller stabilizes the Seesaw system and improves angular regulation compared with the standard LQR design.

	\subsection{Algorithms}
	\label{subsec:algorithms}

	Pseudo-algorithms can be written in \LaTeX{} with the \texttt{algorithm} and \texttt{algorithmic} packages.
	An example is shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:var}.
	\begin{algorithm}[H]
		\label{alg:example}
		\caption{Name of the Algorithm}
		\label{alg:var}
		\label{protocol1}
		\begin{algorithmic}[1]
			\STATE Initial instructions
			\FOR{$for-condition$}
			\STATE{Some instructions}
			\IF{$if-condition$}
			\STATE{Some other instructions}
			\ENDIF
			\ENDFOR
			\WHILE{$while-condition$}
			\STATE{Some further instructions}
			\ENDWHILE
			\STATE Final instructions
		\end{algorithmic}
	\end{algorithm}

\section{LQR and Riccati's Equation}\label{app:ARE}
LQR finds the full-state feedback control law $u = -\bar{K}x$.\\
The algebraic Riccati's equation is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ARE}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		Q       & - \bar{P}BR^{-1}B^\top\bar{P} + \bar{P}A + A^\top \bar{P} = 0 \\
		\dot{P} & (t) = P(t) = \bar{P}
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
With $\bar{P}$ symmetric unique positive definite solution of ARE.\\
The gain is given by :
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ARE_gain}
	\bar{K} = R^{-1}B^\top \bar{P}
\end{equation}

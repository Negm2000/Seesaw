\section{Introduction}
Working with underactuated mechanical systems is a classic method for testing advanced control strategies. This project focuses on the Quanser IP02 Linear Base Unit paired with the Seesaw-E module, a setup that creates a complex, single-input multi-output (SIMO) stabilization problem. Because the integrated system contains both Right-Half Plane (RHP) poles and zeros, it exhibits severe Non-Minimum Phase (NMP) behavior. This required incredible precision with frequency separation and pole placement to keep the seesaw balanced without letting the cart crash into the physical track limits.

This report outlines the entire process, from understanding the raw system dynamics to deploying the final controllers. The methodology began by deriving the Euler-Lagrange equations and writing compensation logic for real-world hardware friction. From there, the approach transitioned from classical cascade loop shaping to modern state-space methods, specifically LQR and LQI control. Finally, since linear controllers are only valid near the equilibrium point, the complex lift-up maneuver was addressed. This was formulated as an Optimal Control Problem using CasADi, generating a Time-Varying LQR (TV-LQR) trajectory to safely bring the seesaw body up from its resting stop.

\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Newton's Second Law}

To accurately capture the system dynamics, the effective inertial load must account for both the physical mass of the assembly and the reflected rotational inertia of the motor armature. The total equivalent lumped mass $M_e$ translated along the rail is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	M_e = M_c + M_w + \frac{J_m K_g^2}{r_{mp}^2}
\end{equation}

Applying Newton’s Second Law to the translational motion of the cart, we account for the driving force $F_c$ and the resistive forces of friction. The equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
    F_c = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{eq} \cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $M_e$ is the total equivalent translational mass of the cart assembly.
    \item $B_{eq}$ is the equivalent linear viscous damping coefficient, capturing the lumped dissipative losses across the track, bearings, and gears.
    \item $F_c$ is the driving linear force delivered to the rack by the DC motor pinion.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Actuator Dynamics and Electromechanical Coupling}
The driving force $F_c$ originates from the electrical domain, where the input armature voltage $V_m$ generates an electromagnetic torque. Accounting for back-EMF and armature resistance, the force is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F_c &= \frac{\eta_g  K_g k_t}{R_m r_{mp}} \left( \eta_m \cdot v_m - \frac{K_g k_m \cdot \dot{x}_c}{r_{mp}} \right)\\
        &= \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m - B_{emf} \cdot \dot{x}_c
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Expanding this expression allows the electrical and mechanical dependencies to be cleanly decoupled into a voltage-proportional force gain and an intrinsic electrical damping term, namely $\alpha_f \eta_m$ and $B_{emf}$.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
Substituting the actuator force expression into the mechanical force balance yields the explicit differential equation mapping the control input voltage $V_m(t)$ directly to the cart linear acceleration $\ddot{x}_c(t)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m(t) - B_{emf}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{eq,c}\cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation*}
Grouping the velocity-dependent terms highlights the total open-loop damping present within the system:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_eom}
    \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{tot}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t)
\end{equation}
Where $B_{tot} = B_{eq} + B_{emf}$ represents the combined effective damping coefficient, encapsulating both mechanical friction losses and back-EMF braking effects. This consolidated linear model serves as the analytical foundation for subsequent system identification and control loop synthesis.

\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Euler-Lagrange Equation}
To accurately model the multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) coupled system, the analytical derivation is performed using the energy-based Euler-Lagrange framework. We select the generalized coordinates vector as:
\begin{equation}
    q = \begin{bmatrix}
        x_c \\ \theta
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
First, considering the baseline moment of inertia of the seesaw assembly about its physical pivot point, we define $J_{pivot}$ as:
\begin{equation*}
    J_{pivot} = J_{SW} + M_C \cdot D_C^2
\end{equation*}
The total kinetic energy $T$ of the coupled system accounts for the linear translation of the cart, the pure rotation of the seesaw base, and the non-linear velocity coupling terms arising from the moving reference frame:
\begin{equation}
	T = \frac{1}{2}M_e \cdot \dot{x}_c^2 + \frac{1}{2}(J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2)\cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{1}{2} M_{tot} \cdot x_c^2 \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}
\end{equation}
Where $M_{tot} = M_c + M_w$ represents the total combined active mass of the translating assembly.\\
The system potential energy $V$ is defined relative to the horizontal datum plane, capturing the height variations of both the translating cart mass along the inclined rail and the shift in the center of gravity of the rotating seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	V = g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot sin(\theta) + g( M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C ) \cdot cos(\theta)
\end{equation}
Using these energy definitions, the system Lagrangian is established as $L = T - V$. The explicit equations of motion are generated by applying the Euler-Lagrange operator to each generalized coordinate:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = Q_i
\end{equation}
Where $Q_i$ represents the vector of generalized non-conservative forces and dissipative torques acting on the system. For the translational and rotational coordinates respectively, the formulation expands to:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{x}_c} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_c}       & = - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c +  \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m\\
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{\theta}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} & = - B_{SW} \cdot \dot{\theta}
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
By executing the partial differentiations explicitly, this system yields a set of coupled, non-linear second-order differential equations that govern the global state-space profile of the cart-seesaw plant. These equations serve as the analytical foundation for subsequent linearization, system identification, and model-based controller synthesis.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
By explicitly evaluating the Euler-Lagrange equations, we obtain the coupled, non-linear single-input multi-output (SIMO) dynamic equations for the complete cart-seesaw system. The resulting system models the full electromechanical translation of the cart alongside the rotational dynamics of the supporting seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:seesaw_eom}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} - M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + g M_{tot} \cdot sin(\theta)                 & =   \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m \\
		M_{tot} \cdot \ddot{\theta} \cdot x_c^2 + 2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot cos(\theta) \cdot x_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c & +                            \\
		(J_{SW} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C) \cdot sin(\theta)                             & = 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_m(t)$ is the control input voltage applied to the linear base unit motor, standardizing the actuator parameters derived previously.
    \item The centripetal acceleration component $M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2$ and the Coriolis coupling term $2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}$ represent the highly non-linear dynamic forces acting across the moving reference frames.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Linearization}
Because these coupled non-linear equations are unsuited for traditional linear control framework designs, the system must be linearized around its unstable equilibrium point. We define the nominal operating equilibrium where the cart rests at the center of the track and the seesaw is perfectly horizontal:
\begin{equation*}
    (x_{c,0}, \theta_0) = (0,0)
\end{equation*}
To perform a small-signal linearization around this equilibrium state, the following assumptions are enforced:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Small angles ($\theta \simeq 0$) : $sin(\theta) = \theta$ and $cos(\theta) = 1$
	\item Small displacement ($x_c \simeq 0$)
	\item Ignoring high-order velocity terms : $\dot{\theta}^2, x_c^2, \dot{x}_c\dot{\theta} \simeq 0$
\end{enumerate}
Applying these assumptions eliminates the non-linear coupling constraints and simplifies the framework into a set of linearly coupled differential equations. This linearized model describes the small-signal perturbations of the system near the origin:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \\
            - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot \theta  
            & =  \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m\\
		- M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c + g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \\
            + (J_{sw} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{sw}D_C) \cdot \theta 
            &= 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
This finalized linear differential model forms the definitive analytical baseline for executing transfer function derivations, state-space mapping, and closed-loop controller synthesis throughout the remainder of this report.

\subsection{Non-linearities Mitigation}
During the experimental validation of the IP02 linear base unit, discrepancies emerged between the idealized linear models and the physical hardware response. These discrepancies are primarily driven by localized, hard non-linearities—specifically static friction, Coulomb friction, and actuator deadzones inherent to the rack-and-pinion and planetary gearbox assembly.

Left uncompensated, these non-linearities introduce steady-state tracking errors, limit cycles, and severe phase distortions that degrade linear controller performance. To restore the validity of the linear assumptions, a data-driven system identification routine was executed to map these non-linear profiles, followed by the implementation of a software-based feedforward inverse-deadband compensation scheme.

The underlying mathematical model for this non-symmetric voltage deadzone is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
V_{eff} = f(V_{cmd}) = 
\begin{cases} 
V_{cmd} + u_{d,pos} & \text{if } V_{cmd} > \epsilon \\ 
0 & \text{if } |V_{cmd}| \le \epsilon \\ 
V_{cmd} - u_{d,neg} & \text{if } V_{cmd} < -\epsilon 
\end{cases}
\label{eq:deadzone_model}
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\epsilon$ is the deadzone threshold, used to avoid chattering between positive and negative motion.
    \item $V_{cmd}$ is the raw voltage command generated by the control architecture.
    \item $V_{eff}$ is the effective, torque-producing voltage delivered to the motor armature after overcoming friction.
    \item $u_{d,pos}$ and $u_{d,neg}$ represent the distinct positive and negative voltage thresholds required to break static friction.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Characterization via Dynamic Modular Signaling}
To cleanly decouple velocity-dependent Coulomb friction from zero-velocity stiction, the system was excited using a structured modular input signal designed to sweep through multiple distinct dynamic phases. This approach guarantees that the cart transitions smoothly between static equilibrium, rapid acceleration profiles, and steady-state velocity regimes, exposing the underlying non-symmetric nature of the voltage deadzone.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Deadzone Extraction}
The script \texttt{seesaw\_nonlinear\_model.m} processes the raw hardware dataset gathered via QUARC to isolate these thresholds. The identification pipeline executes across the following key stages:

\paragraph{Zero-Phase Velocity Estimation}
To circumvent the severe phase-lag and noise amplification associated with raw numerical differentiation, the cart position coordinate ($x_c$) is first pre-processed using a forward-backward digital filter (2nd-order Butterworth via MATLAB's \texttt{filtfilt}). The cutoff frequency ($\omega_c$) is dynamically scaled relative to the system's structural bandwidth, defined as:
\begin{equation}
\omega_c = 2 \cdot \frac{B_{tot}}{M_e}
\label{eq:cutoff_freq}
\end{equation}
The filtered position array is then differentiated with respect to the hardware sampling time to extract a clean velocity profile ($\dot{x}_c$).

\paragraph{Sustained-Motion Masking}
Active velocity states are isolated from measurement noise and micro-oscillations by enforcing a strict kinematic threshold constraint:
\begin{equation}
|\dot{x}_c| > v_{thresh} = 0.002 \, [\text{m/s}]
\end{equation}
A logical persistence routine (masking) ensures that only prolonged, continuous-motion vectors are used for parameter estimation, completely stripping out transient encoder ticks.

\paragraph{Asymmetric Parameter Mapping}
The algorithm splits the valid motion index vectors into directional groups to capture hardware asymmetry. By assessing the minimum absolute input voltage applied during these validated windows, the precise directional deadzone boundaries are computed:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_{d,pos} &= \min(|V_{cmd}|) \quad \forall \left\{k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \mid \dot{x}_c(k) > v_{thresh} \land V_{cmd}(k) > 0\right\} \\
u_{d,neg} &= \min(|V_{cmd}|) \quad \forall \left\{k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \mid \dot{x}_c(k) < -v_{thresh} \land V_{cmd}(k) < 0\right\}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Software-Based Inverse-Deadband Compensation}
Using the extracted parameters stored in \texttt{param\_nonlinear.mat}, an online feedforward compensation block was placed directly upstream of the Digital-to-Analog Converters (DAC). This software block applies an algebraic inverse mapping function to the controller's synthesized voltage output ($V_{ctrl}$) before routing the signal to the power amplifier:
\begin{equation}
V_{DAC} = 
\begin{cases} 
V_{ctrl} + u_{d,pos} & \text{if } V_{ctrl} > \epsilon \\ 
0 & \text{if } V_{ctrl} = 0 \\ 
V_{ctrl} - u_{d,neg} & \text{if } V_{ctrl} < -\epsilon 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
By injecting this step-discontinuity at the zero-crossing point, the controller effectively "pre-shifts" the control effort, neutralizing the actuator deadband. This mathematical cancellation linearizes the input-to-state tracking response, ensuring that the cart's physical translation mirrors the synthesized linear state-space expectations during highly dynamic, bidirectional maneuvers.

\section{Dynamic Modeling \& System Identification}

\subsection{Cart Analytical Modeling: Transfer Function Definition}
Based on the equations of motion derived in the previous section, we define the open-loop transfer function of the isolated cart system. Applying the Laplace transform under the assumption of zero initial conditions yields the transfer function mapping the motor armature voltage $V_m(s)$ to the cart position $X_c(s)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_f \eta_m \cdot V_m(s) &= [M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s] \cdot X_c(s) \\
G_x(s) &= \frac{X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tf_position}
\end{equation}

Because the position transfer function contains a pure integrator, it exhibits an infinite static gain. Consequently, the system identification pipeline leverages the velocity transfer function, $G_v(s)$, which forms a strictly stable, first-order LTI system:
\begin{equation}
G_v(s) = \frac{s \cdot X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{M_e \cdot s + B_{tot}}
\label{eq:tf_velocity}
\end{equation}

This velocity plant features a single stable pole located at $p_{cart} = -B_{tot}/M_e$. Its fundamental dynamic characteristics are defined by its steady-state DC gain, $K_{DC}$, and its time constant, $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
K_{DC} = \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{B_{tot}}, \quad \tau = \frac{M_e}{B_{tot}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cart Parameter Estimation \& Open-Loop Time Validation}
While nominal parameters provide a theoretical baseline, physical hardware introduces discrepancies due to mechanical wear, dust accumulation in the bearings, and unmodeled frictional variations. To guarantee the fidelity of the model, a data-driven parameter estimation routine was executed. 

The focus of this tuning is placed strictly on the equivalent viscous damping coefficient ($B_{eq}$) and the planetary gearbox efficiency ($\eta_g$), as mechanical parameters are highly susceptible to degradation compared to stable electromagnetic constants (like $R_m$ and $\eta_m$).

\subsubsection{Methodology}
The system was excited on the hardware using a 2.0 V step input. To accurately isolate the velocity states without introducing numerical phase lag, the raw encoder position data was differentiated in post-processing. A zero-phase, 2nd-order Butterworth filter (\texttt{filtfilt} in MATLAB) was applied, dynamically tuned relative to the theoretical structural bandwidth ($\omega_c = 2 \cdot B_{tot}/M_e$).

Parameter identification was formulated as an unconstrained non-linear optimization problem. Using the native MATLAB minimization algorithm (\texttt{fminsearch}), the routine minimizes a cost function defined by the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the measured hardware velocity and the simulated response:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\eta_g, B_{eq}} J = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( \dot{x}_{c,hw}(k) - \dot{x}_{c,sim}(k) \right)^2 + \Phi(\eta_g)
\label{eq:cost_function}
\end{equation}
Where $\Phi(\eta_g)$ corresponds to a severe barrier penalty acting as a soft constraint, bounding the gearbox efficiency between 50\% and 110\% of its nominal datasheet value to ensure physical realism. This data-driven tuning minimizes the tracking error globally, locking the refined values of $B_{eq}$ and $\eta_g$ for all subsequent controller syntheses.

\subsection{Cart Open-Loop Frequency Validation}
To synthesize a stable and robust control scheme for the seesaw cascade architecture, the inner-loop cart model must be accurately characterized across a wide frequency spectrum.

\subsubsection{Methodology: Schroeder-Phased Multi-Sine Injection}
A frequency sweep was conducted using a Schroeder-phased multi-sine signal rather than a traditional chirp. This approach allows for the simultaneous injection of $N=10$ logarithmically spaced frequencies, capturing the entire target spectrum efficiently.

Crucially, Schroeder phasing minimizes the crest factor of the composite signal by computing specific phase shifts ($\phi_k$) for each frequency component $k$:
\begin{equation}
\phi_k = -\frac{\pi k (k-1)}{N}
\end{equation}
This maximizes the energy injected at each target frequency without violating the absolute peak voltage budget ($V_{max\_budget} = 3.0$ V) or generating excessive mechanical shear forces at the rack and pinion.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Frequency Limits and Safety Checklist}
To guarantee operational safety, the frequency bandwidth $[\omega_{min}, \omega_{max}]$ was computed dynamically relative to the hardware limits:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower Bound ($\omega_{min}$):} Computed analytically by finding the exact frequency where the maximum voltage budget generates a peak displacement equal to 75\% of the half-track length ($0.75 \cdot T_c/2$) in any direction, safely preventing end-stop collisions.
    \item \textbf{Upper Bound ($\omega_{max}$):} The ideal target is set to $1.5\times$ the bandwidth of the required inner loop. However, the script actively evaluates the force transfer function ($G_{force}$) against the empirically established mechanical clicking limit. If the theoretical required force exceeds the gear-shear threshold, the maximum frequency is strictly capped.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coupled Cart-Seesaw Analytical Modeling: Transfer Function Definition}
To analyze the internal dynamics of the outer loop (angle control), we derive the transfer function mapping the cart's position $x_c$ to the seesaw's angle $\theta$. Deriving the Laplace transform from the linearized coupled equations of motion, assuming zero initial conditions, yields the following matrix relationship:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} 
M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s & -M_{tot}D_T \cdot s^2 + gM_{tot} \\ 
-M_{tot}D_T \cdot s^2 + gM_{tot} & (J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2) \cdot s^2 + B_{SW} \cdot s - g(M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C) 
\end{bmatrix} 
\begin{bmatrix} X_c(s) \\ \Theta(s) \end{bmatrix} 
= \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_f \eta_m \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} V_m(s)
\label{eq:coupled_matrix}
\end{equation}

By isolating the second equation, which represents the purely rotational dynamics of the seesaw base, we extract the explicit transfer function $G_\theta(s)$ mapping the cart displacement to the angular tilt:
\begin{equation}
G_\theta(s) = \frac{\Theta(s)}{X_c(s)} = \frac{M_{tot}D_T \cdot s^2 - gM_{tot}}{(J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2) \cdot s^2 + B_{SW} \cdot s - g(M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C)}
\label{eq:tf_theta}
\end{equation}
This transfer function structurally highlights the system's inherent Non-Minimum Phase (NMP) behavior, dictated by the simultaneous presence of a Right-Half Plane (RHP) pole and a RHP zero resulting from the $-gM_{tot}$ term in the numerator.

\subsection{State-Space Derivation}
For modern control techniques, the system is represented in continuous-time state-space format. We define the standard 4-state vector as $x = [x_c, \theta, \dot{x}_c, \dot{\theta}]^T$.

\subsubsection{Matrix Rewriting and Decoupling}
Rather than computing the highly coupled second-order derivatives manually, the system is decoupled by defining an effective inertia matrix, $M_{eff}$, alongside the state constraint matrix $A_{rhs}$ and input vector $B_{rhs}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
M_{eff} &= \begin{bmatrix} M_e & -M_{tot}D_T \\ -M_{tot}D_T & J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2 \end{bmatrix} \\
A_{rhs} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -gM_{tot} & -B_{tot} & 0 \\ -gM_{tot} & g(M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C) & 0 & -B_{SW} \end{bmatrix} \\
B_{rhs} &= \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_f \eta_m \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The second-order accelerations are computationally resolved via matrix inversion in MATLAB:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \ddot{x}_c \\ \ddot{\theta} \end{bmatrix} = M_{eff}^{-1} A_{rhs} \begin{bmatrix} x_c \\ \theta \\ \dot{x}_c \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix} + M_{eff}^{-1} B_{rhs} V_m
\end{equation}

This mathematical formulation allows the continuous-time state-space matrices ($A_{SW}, B_{SW}, C_{SW}, D_{SW}$) to be extended programmatically, avoiding arithmetic truncation errors:
\begin{equation}
A_{SW} = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{2\times2} & I_{2\times2} \\ M_{eff}^{-1}A_{rhs} \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_{SW} = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{2\times1} \\ M_{eff}^{-1}B_{rhs} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
Because only the position $x_c$ and the angle $\theta$ are directly measurable via the encoders, the output and feedthrough matrices are strictly defined as:
\begin{equation}
C_{SW} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D_{SW} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Augmented System for Integral Action}
To ensure zero steady-state tracking error and robust disturbance rejection during hardware implementation, the standard 4-state model is augmented with a virtual fifth state representing the integral of the seesaw angle:
\begin{equation}
\xi = \int \theta dt
\end{equation}
The augmented state vector becomes $\eta = [x_c, \theta, \dot{x}_c, \dot{\theta}, \xi]^T$, yielding the new matrices $A_5$ and $B_5$ utilized later:
\begin{equation}
A_5 = \begin{bmatrix} A_{SW} & 0_{4\times1} \\ 0 \quad 1 \quad 0 \quad 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_5 = \begin{bmatrix} B_{SW} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Seesaw Parameter Estimation}
While the manufacturer datasheet provides reliable nominal data for the majority of the seesaw parameters, the rotational dynamics are highly sensitive to the exact physical parameter $D_C$ (Distance from the pivot to the Center of Gravity). To mathematically lock this value, a data-driven Quasi-Static Lift-off Test was executed.

\subsubsection{Quasi-Static Methodology}
The physical procedure and software extraction pipeline involves:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Immobilizing the module:} The seesaw is laid against the table at a known cart resting position $x_c(0) \simeq -0.407$ [m] and resting angle $\theta_{rest} \simeq e_{range} = 11.5^\circ$.
    \item \textbf{Quasi-Static Actuation:} A smooth, extremely slow voltage ramp is applied to move the cart toward the pivot, effectively ensuring that the velocity remains negligible ($\dot{x}_c \approx 0, \dot{\theta} \approx 0$), leaving only the gravitational and static torque balances active.
    \item \textbf{Lift-Off Detection:} The data-processing script algorithmically scans the telemetry array to isolate the exact index where the angle breaks from $\theta_{rest}$ by a strict kinematic threshold corresponding to 3 encoder ticks ($3 \cdot K_{E\_SW} / K_{sg} = 0.0015$ rad). The cart's exact coordinate at this threshold is recorded as $x_{lift}$.
\end{enumerate}

At the exact moment of lift-off, the static torques are perfectly balanced. The sum of moments about the pivot yields:
\begin{equation}
g M_{tot} \cdot x_{lift} = g (M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C) \cdot \theta_{rest}
\end{equation}
Rearranging this equilibrium equation isolates the true center of gravity offset:
\begin{equation}
D_C = \left| \frac{M_{tot} \cdot x_{lift} - M_{tot}D_T \cdot \theta_{rest}}{M_{SW} \cdot \theta_{rest}} \right|
\end{equation}
This calculation overwrites the nominal $D_C$ value, directly refining the $A_{SW}$ and $B_{SW}$ matrices prior to feedback controller synthesis.

\section{Frequency-Based Control: Cart Position Control Design}
The strategy employed for this initial framework relies on classical frequency-domain techniques. The objective is to synthesize a feedback controller $C_x(s)$ that shapes the open-loop transfer function $L_x(s) = C_x(s)G_x(s)$ to satisfy specific stability margins and crossover frequencies. Ultimately, this cart position controller serves as the high-bandwidth inner loop within a cascaded architecture designed to stabilize the seesaw angle.

\subsection{Feedback Controller Tuning}
The primary challenge in tuning the inner loop is ensuring its bandwidth is adequately separated from the outer loop's dynamics, which are bounded by the coupled plant's Non-Minimum Phase (NMP) characteristics. The outer loop must be fast enough to stabilize a RHP pole, yet slow enough to avoid excitation of the RHP zero. Taking the geometric mean of these absolute bounds yields a target outer-loop crossover frequency of $\omega_{c,out} \approx 4.45$ rad/s. 

To enforce strict frequency-scale separation, the inner-loop bandwidth target must be 2 to 10 times faster, establishing an acceptable range of $\omega_{c,in} \in (8.9, 44.5)$ rad/s. A mid-range value was consequently selected, setting $\omega_{c,in} = 5 \cdot \omega_{c,out} \approx 22.25$ rad/s. 

Regarding the Phase Margin ($\Phi_{PM,in}$), a standalone feedback loop would typically target $60^\circ$. However, anticipating its role as an inner loop, initial designs aimed to strictly prevent overshoot by targeting a higher margin within the range of $(65^\circ, 90^\circ)$. Following empirical testing, the final phase margin was set to $\Phi_{PM,in} = 65^\circ$. This compromise intentionally restricts the derivative control effort, thereby mitigating the high-frequency noise amplification inherent to the encoder's numerical differentiation.

The selected controller is a PID architecture augmented with a low-pass filter (N-filter) on the derivative term to ensure the transfer function remains strictly proper. The control law is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
C_x(s) = K_{p,in} + \frac{K_{i,in}}{s} + \frac{K_{d,in} \cdot s}{T_{f,in} \cdot s + 1}
\label{eq:pid_inner}
\end{equation}
where $T_{f,in} = 1/N_{in}$.

To analytically extract the required gains, a Frequency Point Shaping with Phase Budget Awareness methodology was utilized. By evaluating the plant's frequency response $G_x(j\omega_{c,in})$ and defining a target controller response $C_{target}(j\omega_{c,in})$ that satisfies the desired crossover magnitude and phase, the proportional and derivative gains are isolated by solving the resulting algebraic system. 

First, the target controller complex vector is defined as:
\begin{equation}
C_{target} = |G_x(j\omega_{c,in})|^{-1} \cdot e^{j \phi_c}
\end{equation}
where the required phase is $\phi_c = -180^\circ + \Phi_{PM,in} - \angle G_x(j\omega_{c,in})$. 

By linking the integrator gain via a conservatively scaled integration time constant ratio ($T_i$) such that $K_{i,in} = K_{p,in}/T_i$, the remaining variables are extracted by separating the real and imaginary components into a linear matrix equation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 & \Re(D(j\omega_{c,in})) \\ 
-\frac{1}{\omega_{c,in} T_i} & \Im(D(j\omega_{c,in})) 
\end{bmatrix} 
\begin{bmatrix} K_{p,in} \\ K_{d,in} \end{bmatrix} 
= \begin{bmatrix} \Re(C_{target}) \\ \Im(C_{target}) \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:pid_matrix}
\end{equation}
where $D(j\omega)$ represents the frequency response of the filtered derivative component. Solving this 2x2 system yields the final hardware-deployable PID coefficients.

\subsubsection{Simulation Validation and Constraints Check}
Because the physical hardware is subject to strict mechanical stress and electronic current limitations, the synthesized controller was rigorously validated in simulation prior to deployment. To attenuate control aggressiveness and limit the initial current surge during step transitions, a second-order Bessel pre-filter $F_{bessel}(s)$ with a cutoff frequency $\omega_{f,bes} = 3.5$ rad/s is applied to shape the position reference signal:
\begin{equation}
F_{bessel}(s) = \frac{3 \cdot \omega_{f,bes}^2}{s^2 + 3 \cdot \omega_{f,bes} \cdot s + 3 \cdot \omega_{f,bes}^2}
\end{equation}

The simulation explicitly incorporates the non-linear friction compensation logic to verify the absolute physical limits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electronic Constraints:} Verifying that the peak armature voltage strictly avoids the amplifier saturation limit ($V_{sat}$) and that peak transient currents remain well under the 3.0 A hardware fault threshold.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical Constraints:} Ensuring the peak dynamic force generated across the rack-and-pinion does not exceed the empirically defined clicking limit, preventing destructive gear shear.
    \item \textbf{Thermal Constraints:} Confirming that the RMS current over the sustained maneuver remains below the continuous thermal rating of 1.0 A.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inner-Loop Re-Tuning}

To ensure hardware survivability and provide smoother, more stable seesaw-angle reference tracking, the inner loop was re-tuned using a less aggressive Frequency Point Shaping methodology.\\

The target crossover frequency was strictly scaled down to $2.5$ times the theoretical outer-loop bandwidth ($\omega_{c,in} = 2.5 \cdot \omega_{c,out} \approx 11.1$ rad/s), rather than the original factor of 5. Additionally, the Phase Margin constraint was increased from $65^\circ$ to $85^\circ$ to absolutely minimize transient overshoot. To further attenuate high-frequency control effort, the derivative filter coefficient was relaxed to $N_{in} = 100$, and a slower integrator ratio ($T_{i,ratio} = 10$) was selected. Finally, a second-order Bessel pre-filter with a cutoff frequency $\omega_{f,bes} = 3.5$ rad/s was implemented on the position reference to prevent initial current surges during step transitions. 

\subsection{Cascade Architecture and System Limitations}
To satisfy both stability and performance criteria for the highly coupled cart-seesaw system, a cascade control architecture is employed. The physical dynamics are decoupled into two hierarchical loops: a high-bandwidth inner loop tracking cart position ($x_c$), and a slower outer loop dictating the required cart position to stabilize the seesaw angle ($\theta$).

The effective plant visible to the outer-loop controller, denoted $P_{out}(s)$, is the series combination of the closed inner-loop transfer function $T_{in}(s)$ and the cart-to-angle cross-coupling dynamics $G_{\theta}(s)$:
\begin{equation}
P_{out}(s) = T_{in}(s)G_{\theta}(s)
\end{equation}

The uncompensated plant exhibits severe Non-Minimum Phase (NMP) behavior, featuring both a Right-Half Plane (RHP) pole and a RHP zero. These structural characteristics impose conflicting theoretical bounds on the achievable closed-loop bandwidth, necessitating highly precise dominant-pole placement to outrun the unstable exponential drift without exacerbating the RHP zero's initial undershoot.

\subsection{Outer-Loop Feedback Controller Tuning}
Because heuristic PID tuning is insufficient for stabilizing this NMP system, a targeted phase-deficit synthesis methodology was utilized. The objective is to compute exact gains for a Lead Compensator (PD) and a PI-Lead Compensator (PID) to lock the dominant closed-loop pole pair at a natural frequency $\omega_n = 4.3$ rad/s with a damping ratio $\zeta = 0.6$:
\begin{equation}
s_d = -\zeta\omega_n + j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}
\end{equation}

A standard root locus analysis of $P_{out}(s)$ indicates that the RHP pole and RHP zero heavily attract one another. Applying a positive proportional gain immediately drives the closed-loop poles deeper into the RHP, as illustrated below.

\subsubsection{Constant Bias Torque and Integral Action Requirement}
While the PD compensator successfully stabilizes the seesaw around the horizontal equilibrium during standard transient events, it inherently lacks the low-frequency gain required to reject constant input disturbances. If an asymmetric load—such as a 100 g offset mass—is applied to the module, the proportional effort is eventually balanced by the disturbance torque, forcing the system to settle at a non-zero steady-state angular tilt.

To actively neutralize this residual error, the architecture is upgraded to a PI-Lead (PID) topology by introducing integral action. An integrator zero is placed exactly a decade below the derivative pole ($z_i = 2.5$ rad/s) to preserve the carefully tuned transient shape established by the Lead compensator.
\begin{equation}
C_{\theta,PID}(s) = -K_{pos} \frac{(s + z_{lead})(s + z_i)}{s(s + p_{lead})}
\end{equation}
This integrator continuously accumulates the steady-state error, ramping the commanded cart displacement until the disturbance is perfectly canceled and the seesaw is forced back to a strict $0^\circ$ equilibrium.

\subsection{Frequency Validation: Reference Tracking Protocol}
The final cascaded PID was then evaluated on the same reference-tracking protocol later used for pole placement and LQR. The run combines $\pm1^\circ$ steps and a short pulse with a stepped sine sweep from $0.10$ to $10$~Hz. This single protocol gives both a transient comparison and a small-signal frequency-response estimate without injecting an unsafe broadband command into the hardware.

Rather than utilizing a multi-sine approach, the protocol automatically injects a continuously concatenated sequence of analytical commands, $r_{\theta}(t)$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline \& Step Analysis:} Initial static regulation followed by $\pm 1.0^\circ$ step commands and a 0.3-second, $1.0^\circ$ impulse to characterize transient settling times and integral windup rejection.
    \item \textbf{Stepped Sine Sweep:} A series of logarithmically spaced sinusoidal tracking commands spanning from $0.10$ Hz to $10.00$ Hz.
\end{enumerate}

To ensure hardware survivability during the high-frequency sine sweeps, two critical signal-processing safeguards were algorithmically embedded into the reference trajectory:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Amplitude Tapering:} Below 2.5 rad/s, the reference amplitude is held at $0.65^\circ$ to reliably overcome static friction. Above this threshold, the amplitude is aggressively tapered proportional to $1/\omega^2$. This guarantees that the necessary acceleration forces acting on the cart rack remain constant and safe, despite the exponentially increasing frequency.
    \item \textbf{Smooth Cyclic Enveloping:} Each frequency segment runs for exactly 7 cycles. The first cycle is multiplied by a half-cosine ramp-up envelope, and the final cycle utilizes a half-cosine ramp-down envelope. This prevents discontinuous velocity shocks at the boundary transitions between differing frequencies.
\end{itemize}

\section{State-Space Control Design: Pole Placement}
\subsection{Full-State Feedback}
\label{sec:pole_placement}

The state-space control strategy utilizes the full-state feedback law $V_{m} = -Kx$, leveraging the continuous-time 4-state plant derived in the modeling section. The primary control objective is to regulate the seesaw angle such that $\theta \rightarrow 0$, with the cart's translation serving strictly to maintain this angular balance. For hardware implementation, only the cart position $x_{c}$ and the seesaw angle $\theta$ are directly measured via encoders; the velocity states $\dot{x}_{c}$ and $\dot{\theta}$ are estimated using an observer.

\subsubsection{Baseline Stabilizing Design}
The initial stabilization attempt (Attempt 1) establishes a baseline controller by mirroring the unstable pole directly into the Left-Half Plane (LHP) while maintaining the remaining poles at their original open-loop locations. 

The desired pole locations and resulting feedback gain vector are:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    P_{des} &= \{-0.42, -2.25, -2.61, -36.50\} \\
    K_{1} &= [78.36, -59.81, 4.46, -22.90]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

While this baseline design successfully stabilizes the system in simulation, the transient response is excessively sluggish. 

\subsubsection{Limit-Cycle Minimization \& Dominant-Pole Redesign}
To address the sluggish transient response and minimize the steady-state limit cycle caused by hardware non-linearities (Coulomb friction, encoder quantization, gear backlash), the controller was redesigned. The closed-loop poles were strategically placed to enforce a specific frequency-domain separation between the cart and the seesaw dynamics.

To outrun the divergence while preventing control efforts that would saturate the 6 V actuator limit, the design enforces a strict hierarchy: the complex conjugate pair governing the seesaw angle ($\theta$) acts as the dominant mode, while the real poles governing the cart are placed at higher frequencies to maintain a $\approx 2:1$ bandwidth window.

The selection parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dominant Angular Pair ($p_{1}, p_{2}$):} A damping ratio of $\zeta_{\theta} = 0.8$ was selected to balance settling time and stability margins. The real part ($\sigma_{\theta}$) dictates the error correction rate and was set to 5.5 rad/s to confidently outpace the open-loop instability. 
    \item \textbf{Cart Velocity Pole ($p_{3}$):} Placed at $1.5 \cdot \sigma_{\theta}$ to guarantee that the cart velocity settles faster than the balancing mode.
    \item \textbf{Cart Position Pole ($p_{4}$):} Placed at $1.2 \cdot \sigma_{\theta}$ to maintain the dominance of the angular pair while setting a reliable return-to-center time constant.
\end{itemize}

This logic yields the following analytical pole placements:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    p_{1,2} &= -5.50 \pm j4.125 \\
    p_{3} &= -8.25 \\
    p_{4} &= -6.60
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

While proportional state-feedback ensures transient stability, it fails to eliminate steady-state error under persistent asymmetric loads. During a simulated bias test representing a 100 g off-center mass, the system slightly shifts its oscillation center away from $\theta = 0^\circ$, exhibiting a constant residual tilt.

To eliminate this steady-state tracking error, the controller utilizes the augmented 5-state architecture ($A_{5}, B_{5}$) introduced in the state-space derivation, leveraging the integral of the angle error ($\xi = \int \theta dt$). 

To preserve the fine-tuned transient shape established by the dominant pair, the four existing poles are retained, and the new integral pole ($p_{int}$) is placed conservatively at a slower frequency of $-0.5$ rad/s. The remaining motion is best interpreted as a friction/quantization limit cycle rather than a failure to stabilize: the open-loop unstable plant is held near the horizontal equilibrium and does not drift.

\subsection{State Estimator: Luenberger Observer}
Because raw numerical differentiation of the encoder ticks heavily amplifies quantization noise, a full-order Luenberger observer is implemented to estimate the unmeasured velocity states robustly.

The observer gain matrix $L$ is computed by assigning the estimator poles to be strictly faster than the controller poles. A proportional scaling factor of $k_{obs} = 5.0$ is applied to the real components of the desired closed-loop poles. 

To prevent the `place` algorithm from failing due to exact pole multiplicities (which degrade numerical computation), a microscopic separation delta ($\delta = 0.1$) is systematically applied to the target real poles:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    p_{obs} = \begin{bmatrix}
        5.0 \cdot \text{Re}(p_{des, 1}) + \delta \\
        5.0 \cdot \text{Re}(p_{des, 2}) - \delta \\
        5.0 \cdot p_{des, 3} + \delta \\
        5.0 \cdot p_{des, 4} - \delta 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{State-Space Control Design: Linear Quadratic Regulator}

\subsection{Optimal Full-State Feedback Synthesis (LQR)}
While the pole-placement technique allows for explicit dynamic shaping, it relies heavily on heuristic iterations to balance robust tracking against actuator saturation. To establish a more mathematically rigorous guarantee of stability margins and effort minimization, a Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) is implemented.

The LQR algorithm computes the optimal full-state feedback gain matrix $K$ by minimizing a quadratic cost function $J$, based on the continuous-time Algebraic Riccati Equation:
\begin{equation}
J = \int_{0}^{\infty} (x^T Q x + u^T R u) dt
\end{equation}

The dynamic behavior of the system is explicitly tuned by balancing two user-defined weighting matrices: the state error penalty $Q$ and the actuator effort penalty $R$.

\subsubsection{Tuning via Bryson's Rule}
Rather than relying on arbitrary diagonal weights, the penalty matrices were constructed using Bryson's Rule. This methodology scales the penalties inversely proportional to the square of the maximum acceptable deviation for each state and input, ensuring that the cost function normalizes the state variables based on strict physical and electrical design targets.

The rule is defined as:
\begin{equation}
Q_{ii} = \frac{1}{\max(|x_{i,dev}|)^2}, \quad R = \frac{1}{\max(|V_{m,dev}|)^2}
\end{equation}

Based on the required tracking tolerances and a conservative voltage budget for the actuator, the maximum acceptable deviations were established as shown in Table \ref{table:bryson_targets}.

\hline

\hline \hline

By leaving the angular speed unconstrained ($+\infty$), the optimizer effectively applies a zero-weight penalty, allowing the seesaw to swing as quickly as mathematically necessary to catch a sudden fall without artificially inflating the cost function.

Applying Bryson's Rule yields the fully optimized state penalty matrix $Q$ and effort penalty scalar $R$:
\begin{equation}
Q_4 = \text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{0.05^2}, \frac{1}{(\pi/180)^2}, \frac{1}{0.1^2}, 0\right), \quad R_4 = \frac{1}{3^2} \approx 0.111
\end{equation}

This methodology guarantees that the LQR natively respects the conservative 3.0 V actuator limit while prioritizing the tightest possible angular balance.

\subsection{Simulation Validation}
To mathematically evaluate the transient capabilities of the synthesized LQR before hardware deployment, two distinct simulated stress tests were conducted.

First, the simulation was subjected to a $1^\circ$ initial-tilt impulse disturbance. The standard 4-state LQR cleanly arrests the angular velocity and returns the seesaw to $0^\circ$ without excessive cart travel or voltage saturation, successfully validating the chosen Bryson parameters for standard dynamic tracking.

When subjected to a simulated 100 g asymmetric bias load (equivalent to a 0.123 Nm continuous torque), the standard LQR successfully avoids total instability but settles at a persistent steady-state tilt error.

\subsection{LQI: Integral Action for Disturbance Rejection}
To entirely eliminate the residual error identified during the simulation validation, the architecture is upgraded to a Linear Quadratic Integral (LQI) controller, utilizing the augmented 5-state system ($\dot{\xi} = \theta$).

The weighting matrix $Q_5$ incorporates the original Bryson parameters while appending a penalty specifically for the integral state. The acceptable deviation for the integral was defined as $4.0^\circ \cdot$ s, representing the maximum time-area allowed to clear a given angular deviation before heavy penalties apply:
\begin{equation}
Q_5 = \text{diag}\left(Q_{11}, Q_{22}, Q_{33}, Q_{44}, \frac{1}{(4 \cdot \pi/180)^2}\right)
\end{equation}

Applying the 100 g bias load against this augmented LQI controller in simulation demonstrates total rejection of the constant disturbance, forcing the steady-state angular error perfectly back to $0^\circ$.

\subsection{Time Validation: Horizontal Stability}
Following theoretical and simulated validation, the LQI controller was deployed to the physical hardware using the same dirty-derivative velocity reconstruction as the pole-placement implementation. With the reference held at $0^\circ$, the loop holds the body to approximately $0.42^\circ$ RMS. This is slightly looser than pole placement but substantially quieter than the cascaded PID, while requiring essentially the same RMS voltage as pole placement ($0.58$~V). A dedicated disturbance run also recovered to the same regulation band after manual perturbations, confirming that the controller stabilizes the plant robustly around the horizontal equilibrium.

\subsection{State Estimator: Kalman Filter (LQE)}
To estimate the unmeasured velocity states robustly, a Linear Quadratic Estimator (LQE) or Kalman Filter replaces the filtered derivative. The Kalman Filter computes the optimal observer gain $L$ by treating the system stochastically,  balancing expected modeling inaccuracies against known sensor precision.

The estimator utilizes two covariance matrices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Process Noise Covariance ($Q_n$):} Represents the uncertainty in the mathematical plant model (e.g., unmodeled friction, backlash).
    \item \textbf{Measurement Noise Covariance ($R_n$):} Represents the electrical and quantization noise present in the physical sensors. This has been fixed to one encoder tick.
\end{itemize}

Because the incremental encoders provide high-fidelity positional measurements, while the dynamic velocity transitions are heavily subject to unmodeled frictional variations, the matrices were structured to mostly trust the sensors:
\begin{equation}
Q_n = \text{diag}(10^{-7}, 7.61 \times 10^{-7}, 10^{-6}, 10^{-6}), \quad R_n = \text{diag}(2.27 \times 10^{-5}, 1.5 \times 10^{-3})
\end{equation}

Because the ratio $Q_n / R_n$ is structurally tuned, the Kalman filter converges on an optimal gain matrix $L$ that minimizes the variance of the estimation error in the linear stochastic model.

A clean hardware validation of the Kalman filter is not yet established. In the deployed runs, the feedback loop was closed on dirty-derivative velocities and the estimator channels were logged only in parallel. The available logs do not consistently show the internal checks expected from a validated estimator: the candidate angular-rate estimate does not reliably match the derivative of its own angle estimate, and the innovation is not convincingly white. The design is therefore retained as a theoretically sound estimator, but its on-hardware performance should be treated as future work until it is run and validated in the loop.

\section{Advanced Non-Linear Maneuver: Seesaw Lift-up}

The lift-up problem represents the most complex control challenge for the cart-seesaw system because the motion initiates from a resting position on the mechanical stop. The specific initial condition is located far from the horizontal equilibrium:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    x(0) &= [-0.407\text{ m}, \, 11.66^\circ, \, 0, \, 0]^{T}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Because the linear controllers synthesized in previous sections are strictly valid only near the horizontal equilibrium ($\theta = 0^\circ$), they cannot be directly engaged from this resting state without causing severe instability or actuator saturation. To bridge this gap, we first analyze the safe operating boundaries of the linear controllers (the Region of Attraction), and subsequently design strategies to drive the system from the mechanical stop into this safe zone.

\subsection{Region of Attraction and Lyapunov Bounding}
To determine exactly when it is safe to activate a linear controller, we must compute its Region of Attraction (ROA). The ROA defines the subset of the state-space from which the closed-loop system is guaranteed to converge asymptotically to the equilibrium without violating any physical or electrical constraints.

For a linear state-feedback system $\dot{x} = A_{cl}x$, we define a common quadratic Lyapunov function:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    V(x) &= x^{T} P x
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The symmetric, positive-definite matrix $P$ characterizes the shape of the energy ellipsoid. The method to obtain $P$ depends strictly on the control strategy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For LQR/LQI:} The $P$ matrix is natively obtained as the unique stabilizing solution to the continuous-time Algebraic Riccati Equation (ARE) used to synthesize the gains.
    \item \textbf{For Pole Placement:} Because the gains are placed explicitly without an underlying cost function, $P$ is found by selecting an arbitrary positive-definite weight matrix $Q$ (typically the identity matrix, $Q=I$, but this can be optimized) and solving the continuous Lyapunov equation:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        A_{cl}^{T} P + P A_{cl} + Q &= 0
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Once $P$ is computed, the ROA is defined as the largest bounded level set $V(x) \le c^*$ that strictly obeys the system's operational limits (maximum cart travel $x_{max} = 0.432$ m, maximum angle $\theta_{max} = 11.66^\circ$, and maximum actuator voltage $V_{max} = 6.0$ V, maximum slew rate $60$ V/s). 

Mathematically, for any generic linear constraint defined as $|H x| \le b$, the maximum allowable energy level $c$ that strictly avoids violating this limit is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    c &= \frac{b^2}{H P^{-1} H^{T}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The global bound $c^*$ is simply the absolute minimum of the individual bounds evaluated across all positional, angular, and input constraints. 

The hardware staircase test in the LQR section shows that the local controller can still stabilize steep references when the command is increased slowly: the upward leg remained stable up to $10^\circ$, close to the $11.66^\circ$ mechanical limit. This does not mean the linear controller can perform lift-up from rest; it only shows that the capture region is practically large when the state is already moving quasi-statically near equilibrium.

\subsection{Heuristic Lift-up via Gain Attenuation}
Prior to formulating a fully optimized trajectory, an initial empirical lift-up maneuver was successfully executed with our "worst" state-space technique, relying on the premise that "who can do less can do more."

Rather than engaging the full aggressive effort of the unaugmented Pole Placement controller from the resting state, the maneuver begins by applying an artificially attenuated gain profile. By globally scaling the feedback gains down to 30\% of their nominal values, the system injects just enough energy to induce a slow, gentle lift-off without triggering violent actuator saturation or destabilizing the cart reference. 

As the attenuated control effort swings the seesaw upward, the telemetry is continuously monitored. The exact moment the state vector crosses the mathematical boundary into the pre-computed Region of Attraction ($x^T P x < c^*$), the software immediately switches to the 100\% nominal gain controller. Once inside the ROA, the standard controller safely arrests the residual momentum and locks the seesaw into horizontal equilibrium.

\subsection{Theoretical Improvement: Trajectory Optimization \& TV-LQR}
To replace the heuristic approach with a mathematically rigorous and optimal maneuver, a trajectory optimization protocol paired with a Time-Varying Linear Quadratic Regulator (TV-LQR) was designed. This protocol actively computes and tracks a continuous path from the resting mechanical stop directly to the LQI capture region.

\subsubsection{Nonlinear Trajectory Optimization (CasADi)}
The optimal lift-up is formulated as an Optimal Control Problem (OCP) using the CasADi framework. Rather than decoupling the cart motion, the optimizer leverages the fully coupled system dynamics, driving the system from the initial resting state directly to the stabilized origin:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    x_{start} &= [-0.407\text{ m}, \, 11.66^\circ, \, 0, \, 0]^{T} \\
    x_{end} &\approx [0, \, 0, \, 0, \, 0]^{T}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The trajectory is discretized over $N=100$ intervals using a 4th-order Runge-Kutta integrator. The optimizer minimizes a cost function that balances the total maneuver time ($T$) against the cumulative electrical control effort ($U$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    J_{OCP} &= w_{T} \cdot T + w_{U} \int_{0}^{T} U(t)^2 \,dt
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $w_{T} = 1$ and $w_{U} = 0.1$. 

To guarantee hardware safety, the OCP enforces strict operational constraints along the entire generated path: the cart must remain within the track boundaries ($|x_{c}| \le 0.432\text{ m}$), the actuator voltage must not saturate ($|V_{m}| \le 6.0\text{ V}$), and the maneuver must complete within a feasible time window ($0.1 \le T \le 5.0\text{ s}$). 

\subsubsection{Time-Varying LQR (TV-LQR) \& Stationary Catch}
Because tracking a fast, highly non-linear trajectory with a fixed-gain linear controller yields poor reference tracking, a continuous TV-LQR gain schedule is generated to stabilize the feedforward path. 

Using the terminal LQI cost matrix ($P_{end}$) as the boundary condition, the differential Riccati equation is integrated \textit{backward in time} along the optimized state trajectory $x_{tv}(t)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    -\dot{P}(t) &= A(t)^T P(t) + P(t) A(t) - P(t) B(t) R^{-1} B(t)^T P(t) + Q
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This produces a time-varying feedback gain matrix $K_{tv}(t)$. During the simulated maneuver, the applied voltage combines the optimal CasADi feedforward effort with the TV-LQR feedback correction:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    V_{m}(t) &= U_{tv}(t) - K_{tv}(t) \cdot (x(t) - x_{tv}(t))
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

As the seesaw swings upward and crosses near the vertical center, the controller must seamlessly transition to the stationary LQI architecture to maintain perpetual balance. To prevent destructive chattering if the measured angle oscillates across the activation boundary, a one-way flip-flop logic is utilized. The LQI controller permanently takes over once the cart position exceeds $0.057\text{ m}$ and the seesaw angle falls below $5^\circ$.

\subsubsection{Simulation Validation}
Prior to hardware deployment, the full closed-loop lift-up is mathematically validated by the simulation to ensure the trajectory is not only optimal but physically survivable. The script explicitly verifies the following criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kinematic Safety:} The simulated cart position never breaches the mechanical rails ($x_{min}, x_{max}$).
    \item \textbf{Actuator Feasibility:} The closed-loop control effort (feedforward + feedback) strictly remains inside the $\pm 6\text{ V}$ saturation limit.
    \item \textbf{Region of Attraction Capture:} The simulation actively evaluates the Lyapunov function $V(x) = x^{T} P_{end} x$. It mathematically confirms that the state vector securely drops below the threshold bound ($c^*$) \textit{before} the flip-flop logic triggers the switch to the stationary LQI controller.
    \item \textbf{Logic Stability:} The one-way flag \texttt{has\_switched} guarantees a clean, single transition without high-frequency switching noise.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This project developed a complete control workflow for the Quanser IP02 cart and Seesaw-E system, including analytical modeling, parameter identification, controller synthesis, simulation validation, and hardware testing. The nonlinear equations were derived using the Euler--Lagrange method and then linearized around the horizontal equilibrium. Since the real apparatus presented nonideal effects such as Coulomb friction, actuator deadzone, backlash, and sensor noise, model refinement and compensation were necessary before reliable controller implementation.

The experimental work confirmed that the Seesaw is strongly coupled and non-minimum-phase, so the cart and body angle cannot be treated as two independent SISO systems. Classical frequency-based control was useful for building intuition and validating the cart dynamics, while state-space control provided a more suitable framework for balancing the complete coupled system. The most reliable local solution was obtained by combining integral action with state estimation, allowing the controller to reject the steady-state error introduced by the 100 g asymmetric bias load.

The lift-up task showed the limitation of local linear controllers, since they are valid only near the horizontal equilibrium. For this reason, a hybrid nonlinear strategy was proposed: generate a feasible lift-up trajectory, track it with TV-LQR, and switch to the stationary balancing controller once the state enters the safe capture region.

Several practical hardware issues affected the development process. Gear clicking, excessive friction, degraded track behavior, encoder noise, occasional sign anomalies, and QUARC/software cache problems required repeated retuning, validation, and in some cases full system rebooting. These difficulties made the project more challenging but also showed the importance of robust modeling, conservative safety checks, and well-structured MATLAB/Simulink scripts. Overall, the project fulfilled the main objectives of modeling, identification, controller design, implementation, validation, and critical comparison of the developed strategies.

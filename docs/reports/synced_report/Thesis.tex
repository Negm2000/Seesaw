\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Newton's Second Law}

To accurately capture the system dynamics, the effective inertial load must account for both the physical mass of the assembly and the reflected rotational inertia of the motor armature. The total equivalent lumped mass $M_e$ translated along the rail is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	M_e = M_c + M_w + \frac{J_m K_g^2}{r_{mp}^2}
\end{equation}

Applying Newton’s Second Law to the translational motion of the cart, we account for the driving force $F_c$ and the resistive forces of friction. The equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
    F_c = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{eq} \cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $M_e$ is the total equivalent translational mass of the cart assembly.
    \item $B_{eq}$ is the equivalent linear viscous damping coefficient, capturing the lumped dissipative losses across the track, bearings, and gears.
    \item $F_c$ is the driving linear force delivered to the rack by the DC motor pinion.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Actuator Dynamics and Electromechanical Coupling}
The driving force $F_c$ originates from the electrical domain, where the input armature voltage $V_m$ generates an electromagnetic torque. Accounting for back-EMF and armature resistance, the force is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F_c &= \frac{\eta_g  K_g k_t}{R_m r_{mp}} \left( \eta_m \cdot v_m - \frac{K_g k_m \cdot \dot{x}_c}{r_{mp}} \right)\\
        &= \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m - B_{emf} \cdot \dot{x}_c
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Expanding this expression allows the electrical and mechanical dependencies to be cleanly decoupled into a voltage-proportional force gain and an intrinsic electrical damping term, namely $\alpha_f \eta_m$ and $B_{emf}$.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
Substituting the actuator force expression into the mechanical force balance yields the explicit differential equation mapping the control input voltage $V_m(t)$ directly to the cart linear acceleration $\ddot{x}_c(t)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m(t) - B_{emf}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{eq,c}\cdot \dot{x}_c
\end{equation*}
Grouping the velocity-dependent terms highlights the total open-loop damping present within the system:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_eom}
    \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v(t) = M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c(t) + B_{tot}\cdot \dot{x}_c(t)
\end{equation}
Where $B_{tot} = B_{eq} + B_{emf}$ represents the combined effective damping coefficient, encapsulating both mechanical friction losses and back-EMF braking effects. This consolidated linear model serves as the analytical foundation for subsequent system identification and control loop synthesis.

\subsubsection{Analytical Modeling via Euler-Lagrange Equation}
To accurately model the multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) coupled system, the analytical derivation is performed using the energy-based Euler-Lagrange framework. We select the generalized coordinates vector as:
\begin{equation}
    q = \begin{bmatrix}
        x_c \\ \theta
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
First, considering the baseline moment of inertia of the seesaw assembly about its physical pivot point, we define $J_{pivot}$ as:
\begin{equation*}
    J_{pivot} = J_{SW} + M_C \cdot D_C^2
\end{equation*}
The total kinetic energy $T$ of the coupled system accounts for the linear translation of the cart, the pure rotation of the seesaw base, and the non-linear velocity coupling terms arising from the moving reference frame:
\begin{equation}
	T = \frac{1}{2}M_e \cdot \dot{x}_c^2 + \frac{1}{2}(J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2)\cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{1}{2} M_{tot} \cdot x_c^2 \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}
\end{equation}
Where $M_{tot} = M_c + M_w$ represents the total combined active mass of the translating assembly.\\
The system potential energy $V$ is defined relative to the horizontal datum plane, capturing the height variations of both the translating cart mass along the inclined rail and the shift in the center of gravity of the rotating seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	V = g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot sin(\theta) + g( M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C ) \cdot cos(\theta)
\end{equation}
Using these energy definitions, the system Lagrangian is established as $L = T - V$. The explicit equations of motion are generated by applying the Euler-Lagrange operator to each generalized coordinate:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = Q_i
\end{equation}
Where $Q_i$ represents the vector of generalized non-conservative forces and dissipative torques acting on the system. For the translational and rotational coordinates respectively, the formulation expands to:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{x}_c} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_c}       & = - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c +  \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m\\
		\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial t \partial \dot{\theta}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} & = - B_{SW} \cdot \dot{\theta}
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
By executing the partial differentiations explicitly, this system yields a set of coupled, non-linear second-order differential equations that govern the global state-space profile of the cart-seesaw plant. These equations serve as the analytical foundation for subsequent linearization, system identification, and model-based controller synthesis.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
By explicitly evaluating the Euler-Lagrange equations, we obtain the coupled, non-linear single-input multi-output (SIMO) dynamic equations for the complete cart-seesaw system. The resulting system models the full electromechanical translation of the cart alongside the rotational dynamics of the supporting seesaw frame:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:seesaw_eom}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c - B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} - M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2 + g M_{tot} \cdot sin(\theta)                 & =   \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m \\
		M_{tot} \cdot \ddot{\theta} \cdot x_c^2 + 2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot cos(\theta) \cdot x_c - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c & +                            \\
		(J_{SW} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C) \cdot sin(\theta)                             & = 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_m(t)$ is the control input voltage applied to the linear base unit motor, standardizing the actuator parameters derived previously.
    \item The centripetal acceleration component $M_{tot} \cdot x_c \cdot \dot{\theta}^2$ and the Coriolis coupling term $2 M_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \cdot \dot{\theta}$ represent the highly non-linear dynamic forces acting across the moving reference frames.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Linearization}
Because these coupled non-linear equations are unsuited for traditional linear control framework designs, the system must be linearized around its unstable equilibrium point. We define the nominal operating equilibrium where the cart rests at the center of the track and the seesaw is perfectly horizontal:
\begin{equation*}
    (x_{c,0}, \theta_0) = (0,0)
\end{equation*}
To perform a small-signal linearization around this equilibrium state, the following assumptions are enforced:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Small angles ($\theta \simeq 0$) : $sin(\theta) = \theta$ and $cos(\theta) = 1$
	\item Small displacement ($x_c \simeq 0$)
	\item Ignoring high-order velocity terms : $\dot{\theta}^2, x_c^2, \dot{x}_c\dot{\theta} \simeq 0$
\end{enumerate}
Applying these assumptions eliminates the non-linear coupling constraints and simplifies the framework into a set of linearly coupled differential equations. This linearized model describes the small-signal perturbations of the system near the origin:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		M_e \cdot \ddot{x}_c + B_{tot} \cdot \dot{x}_c \\
            - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{\theta} + g M_{tot} \cdot \theta  
            & =  \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot v_m\\
		- M_{tot} D_T \cdot \ddot{x}_c + g M_{tot} \cdot x_c \\
            + (J_{sw} + M_{tot} D^2_T) \cdot \ddot{\theta} + B_{sw} \cdot \dot{\theta} - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{sw}D_C) \cdot \theta 
            &= 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
This finalized linear differential model forms the definitive analytical baseline for executing transfer function derivations, state-space mapping, and closed-loop controller synthesis throughout the remainder of this report.

\subsection{Non-linearities mitigation}
During experimental validation of the IP02, discrepancies emerged between the idealized linear models and the physical hardware response. These discrepancies are primarily driven by localized, hard non-linearities (specifically static friction, Coulomb friction, and actuator deadzones inherent to the rack-and-pinion and planetary gearbox assembly).\\
Left uncompensated, these non-linearities introduce steady-state tracking errors, limit cycles, and severe phase distortions that degrade linear controller performance. To restore the validity of the linear assumptions, a data-driven system identification routine was executed to map these non-linear profiles, followed by the implementation of a software-based feedforward inverse-deadband compensation scheme.\\

The underlying mathematical model for this non-symmetric voltage deadzone is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{eff}} = f(V_{\text{cmd}}) = 
\begin{cases} 
    V_{\text{cmd}} + u_{d,\text{pos}} & \text{if } V_{\text{cmd}} > \epsilon \\
    0 & \text{if } |V_{\text{cmd}}| \le \epsilon \\ 
    V_{\text{cmd}} - u_{d,\text{neg}} & \text{if } V_{\text{cmd}} < -\epsilon \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\epsilon$ is the deadzone threshold, to avoid chattering between positive and negative motion.
    \item $V_{\text{cmd}}$ is the raw voltage command generated by the control architecture.
    \item $V_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective, torque-producing voltage delivered to the motor armature after overcoming friction.
    \item $u_{d,\text{pos}}$ and $u_{d,\text{neg}}$ represent the distinct positive and negative voltage thresholds required to break static friction.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Characterization via Dynamic Modular Signaling}
To cleanly decouple velocity-dependent Coulomb friction from zero-velocity stiction, the system was excited using a structured modular input signal designed to sweep through multiple distinct dynamic phases. This approach guarantees that the cart transitions smoothly between static equilibrium, rapid acceleration profiles, and steady-state velocity regimes, exposing the underlying non-symmetric nature of the voltage deadzone.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Deadzone Extraction}
The script \texttt{seesaw\_nonlinear\_model.m} processes the raw hardware dataset gathered via QUARC to isolate these tresholds. The identification pipeline executes across the following key stages:
\paragraph{Zero-Phase Velocity Estimation}
To circumvent the severe phase-lag and noise amplification associated with raw numerical differentiation, the cart position coordinate ($x_c$) is first pre-processed using a forward-backward digital filter (2nd-order \textit{Butterworth} via texttt{filtfilt}). The cutoff frequency is dynamically scaled relative to the system's structural time constant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:butter_cutoff}
    \omega _{c,butter} = 2 \cdot \frac{B_{tot}}{M_e}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Sustained-Motion Masking}
Active velocity states are isolated from measurement noise and micro-oscillations by enforcing a strict kinematic threshold constraint:
    \begin{equation*}
        |\dot{x}_c| > v_{tresh} = 0.002 [m/s]
    \end{equation*}
    A logical persistence routine ensures that only prolonged, continuous-motion vectors are used for parameter estimation, completely stripping out transient encoder ticks.
\paragraph{Asymmetric Parameter Mapping}
The algorithm splits the valid motion index vectors into directional groups to capture hardware asymmetry. By assessing the minimum absolute input voltage applied during these validated windows, the precise directional deadzone boundaries are computed:
\begin{align*}
    u_{d,\text{pos}} &= \min \left( |V_{\text{diff}}| \right) &\forall \ \{k \ \in \ \mathbb{Z}^+ \ | \ \dot{x}_c(k) > v_{\text{thresh}} \ \land \ V_{\text{diff}}(k) > 0\}\\
    u_{d,\text{neg}} &= \min \left( |V_{\text{diff}}| \right) &\forall \ \{k \ \in \ \mathbb{Z}^+ \ | \ \dot{x}_c(k) < -v_{\text{thresh}} \ \land \ V_{\text{diff}}(k) < 0\}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Software-Based Inverse-Deadband Compensation}
Using the extracted parameters stored in \texttt{param\_nonlinear.mat}, an online feedforward compensation block was placed directly upstream of the Digital-to-Analog Converters (DAC). This software block applies an algebraic inverse mapping function to the controller's synthesized voltage output ($V_{ctrl}$) before routing the signal to the power amplifier:
\begin{equation*}
    V_{\text{DAC}} = \begin{cases} V_{\text{ctrl}} + u_{d,\text{pos}} & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} > \epsilon \\ 0 & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} = 0 \\ V_{\text{ctrl}} - u_{d,\text{neg}} & \text{if } V_{\text{ctrl}} < \epsilon \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
By injecting this step-discontinuity at the zero-crossing point, the controller effectively "pre-shifts" the control effort, neutralizing the actuator deadband. This mathematical cancellation linearizes the input-to-state tracking response, ensuring that the cart's physical translation mirrors the synthesized linear state-space expectations during highly dynamic, bidirectional maneuvers.

\section{Dynamic Modeling \& System Identification}
\subsection{Cart Analytical Modeling: Transfer Function Definition}
Based on the equation of motion derived at Eq.~\ref{eq:cart_eom}, we define the open-loop transfer function of the system. Applying the Laplace transform under the assumption of zero initial condition yields the transfer function mapping the motor armature voltage $V_m(s)$ to the cart position $X_c(s)$.
\begin{equation*}
	\label{eq:cart_eom_laplace}
	\alpha_f \eta_m\cdot V_m(s) = [M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s]\cdot X_c(s)
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_tf}
    G_x(s) = \frac{X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s}
\end{equation}
Because the position transfer function contains a pure integrator, it exhibits an infinite static gain. Consequently, the system identification pipeline leverages the velocity transfer function, $G_v(s)$, which forms a strictly stable, first-order LTI system:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cart_velocity_tf}
    G_v(s) = \frac{s \cdot X_c(s)}{V_m(s)} = \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{M_e \cdot s + B_{tot}}
\end{equation}

This velocity plant features a single stable pole located at $p_{cart} = -B_{tot}/M_e$. Its fundamental dynamic characteristics are defined by its steady-state DC gain, $K_{DC}$, and its time constant, $\tau$.

\begin{align*}
    K_{DC} &= \frac{\alpha_f \eta_m}{B_{tot}} &
    \tau &= \frac{M_e}{B_{tot}}
\end{align*}
\subsection{Cart Parameter Estimation \& Open-Loop Time Validation} \label{sec:cart_model_tuning}
While nominal parameters provide a theoretical baseline, physical hardware introduces discrepancies due to mechanical wear, dust accumulation in the bearings, and unmodeled frictional variations. To guarantee the fidelity of the model, a data-driven parameter estimation routine was executed. Most of the values obtained from Tab.~\ref{table:datasheet} are assumed to be true. The focus will be put on the viscous damping coefficient $B_{eq,w}$ and the planetary gearbox efficiency $\eta_g$.\\
Other parameters have been considered like the motor resistance $R_m$ or the motor efficiency $\eta_m$ but their electromagnetic properties are more stable than the mechanical properties of involved parameters.
\begin{align*}
    K_{DC} &= \frac{\alpha_f (\eta_g) \eta_m}{B_{tot}(\eta_g, B_{eq,w})} &
    \tau &= \frac{M_e}{B_{tot}(\eta_g, B_{eq,w})}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
The system was excited using a $2.0V$ step input. To accurately isolate the velocity states without introducing numerical phase lag, the raw encoder position data was differentiated in post-processing using a zero-phase, 2nd-order Butterworth filter (\texttt{filtfilt} in MATLAB) tuned relative to the theoretical structural bandwidth (see eq.~\ref{eq:butter_cutoff}).\\
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/frog.png}
    \caption{step response hw}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

Parameter identification was formulated as an unconstrained non-linear optimization problem. Using the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm (\texttt{fminsearch} in MATLAB), the routine minimizes a cost function defined by the MSE between hardware data and simulated response.
\begin{equation}
    J(\eta_g,B_{eq,w}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{k=1} ^N \left( \dot{x}_{c,hw}(k) - \dot{x}_{c,hw}(k) \right)^2 + \Phi(\eta_g)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cart Open-Loop Frequency Validation}
To synthesize a stable and robust control scheme for the seesaw, the model must be accurately characterized across a wide frequency spectrum. This section details the empirical frequency-domain validation used to verify the step-response tuning of $G_x(s)$.

\subsubsection{Methodology: Schroeder-Phased Multi-Sine Injection}
A frequency sweep was conducted using a Schroeder-phased multi-sine signal rather than a traditional chirp or stepped-sine approach. This methodology provides several distinct advantages.
\paragraph{Timescale Efficiency:} 
It allows for the simultaneous injection of \( N=10 \) logarithmically spaced frequencies, capturing the entire target spectrum in a single, continuous test window.
\paragraph{Hardware Protection:} 
Schroeder phasing minimizes the crest factor of the composite signal by computing phase shifts $\phi _k$ for each frequency component $k$. This maximizes the energy injected at each target frequency without violating the absolute peak voltage limits (\( V_{max} \)) or generating excessive mechanical shear forces at the rack and pinion.
\begin{equation*}
    \phi _k = - \frac{\pi k (k-1)}{N}
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Frequency Limits}
To guarantee operational safety, the frequency bandwidth $[\omega_{min}, \omega_{max}]$ was computed dynamically relative to the hardware limits:
\paragraph{Lower Bound ($\omega_{min}$)}
Computed analytically by finding the frequency where the maximum voltage budget generates a peak displacement equal to 75\% of the half-track length, preventing catastrophic end-stop collisions.
\paragraph{Upper Bound ($\omega_{max}$)}
Initial goal is 1.5 the estimated inner-loop bandwidth target, but the test is capped safely below the torque limit (computed by trial/errors during the labs)\\

\subsection{Coupled Cart-Seesaw Analytical Modeling: Transfer Function Definition}
To analyze the internal dynamics of the outer§loop (angle control), we derive the transfer function mapping the cart's position $x_c$ to the seesaw's angle $\theta$.
Deriving the Laplace transform from the linearized equations of motion Eq.~\ref{eq:seesaw_eom}, keeping the zero initial conditions assumption:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		(M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot}\cdot s) \cdot X_c(s)                                  & +                         \\
		(- M_{tot} D_T \cdot s^2 + g M_{tot} ) \cdot \Theta (s)                                     & =  \alpha_f \eta_m \cdot V_m(s) \\
		(-M_{tot} D_T \cdot s^2 + g M_{tot}) \cdot X_c(s)                                             & +                         \\
		( (J_{pivot} + M_{tot} D_T^2) \cdot s^2 + B_{SW} \cdot s - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C)) \cdot \Theta (s) & = 0
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}

The coupled dynamics can be expressed in the as the matrix:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		M_e \cdot s^2 + B_{tot} \cdot s & -M_{tot} D_T \cdot s^2 + g M_{tot}                                      \\
		-M_{tot} D_T \cdot s^2 + g M_{tot}            & (J_{pivot} + M_{tot} D_T^2) \cdot s^2 + B_{SW} \cdot s - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW} D_C)
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		X(s) \\
		\Theta(s)
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\alpha_f \eta_m \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
	V_m(s)
\end{equation}

By isolating the second equation, which represents the purely rotational dynamics of the seesaw base, we extract the explicit relationship between the cart's position and the seesaw's angle. This yields the transfer function $G_\theta(s)$:
\begin{equation}
    G_\theta(s) = \frac{\Theta(s)}{X_c(s)} = \frac{M_{tot} D_T \cdot s^2 - g M_{tot}}{(J_{pivot} + M_{tot} D_T^2) \cdot s^2 + B_{sw} \cdot s - g(M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW}D_C)}
\end{equation}
This transfer function highlights the system's inherent non-minimum phase (NMP) behavior, dictated by the presence of a Right-Half Plane (RHP) zero resulting from the $-g M_{tot}$ term in the numerator (alongside the RHP pole).

\subsection{State-Space Derivation}
We define the standard 4-state vector as $x = [x_c, \theta, \dot{x}_c, \dot{\theta}]^T$

\subsubsection{Matrix Rewriting}
\begin{equation}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		M_e     & -M_{tot}D_T           \\
		-M_{tot}D_T & J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\ddot{x}_c \\
		\ddot{\theta}
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0     & -gM_{tot}                 & -B_{tot} & 0       \\
		-gM_{tot} & g(M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C) & 0        & -B_{SW}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c       \\
		\theta    \\
		\dot{x}_c \\
		\dot{\theta}
	\end{bmatrix}
	+ \begin{bmatrix}
		\alpha_f \eta_m \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
	v_m
\end{equation}

Rather than computing the highly coupled second-order derivatives manually, the system is decoupled by defining an effective inertia matrix, $M_{eff}$, alongside the state constraint matrix $A_{rhs}$ and input vector $B_{rhs}$:

\begin{align}
    M_{eff} &= \begin{bmatrix}
		M_e     & -M_{tot}D_T           \\
		-M_{tot}D_T & J_{pivot} + M_{tot}D_T^2
	\end{bmatrix} \\
    A_{rhs} &= \begin{bmatrix}
		0     & -gM_{tot}                 & -B_{tot} & 0       \\
		-gM_{tot} & g(M_{tot}D_T + M_{SW}D_C) & 0        & -B_{SW}
	\end{bmatrix} \\
    B_{rhs} &= \begin{bmatrix}
		\alpha_f \eta_m \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Resolution}
The second-order accelerations are computationally resolved via matrix inversion: 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{x}_c & \ddot{\theta} \end{bmatrix}^T = M_{eff}^{-1} A_{rhs} x + M_{eff}^{-1} B_{rhs} v_m
\end{equation*}
This allows the continuous-time state-space matrices ($A_{sw}$, $B_{sw}$, $C_{sw}$, $D_{sw}$) to be assembled programmatically, eliminating arithmetic truncation errors.\\
We can extend this system to include $\dot{x}_c$ and $\dot{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\dot{x}_c    \\
			\dot{\theta} \\
			\ddot{x}_c   \\
			\ddot{\theta}
		\end{bmatrix}
		 & =
		\begin{bmatrix}
			0_{2 \times 2} & I_{2 \times 2} \\
                M_{eff}^{-1} A_{rhs}
		\end{bmatrix}
		\begin{bmatrix}
			x_c       \\
			\theta    \\
			\dot{x}_c \\
			\dot{\theta}
		\end{bmatrix} 
		 +
		\begin{bmatrix}
			0 _{2 \times 1}  \\
			M_{eff}^{-1} B_{rhs}
		\end{bmatrix}
		v_m
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

This gives the state and input matrices as
\begin{align*}
	A_{SW} & =\begin{bmatrix}
			0_{2 \times 2} &  I_{2 \times 2} \\
                M_{eff}^{-1} A_{rhs} 
		\end{bmatrix} \\
	B_{SW} & = \begin{bmatrix}
			0 _{2 \times 1}  \\
			M_{eff}^{-1} B_{rhs}
		\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
Regarding the output equation, only the positions $x_c$ and the angle $\theta$ are observable and the measurements are not dependent on the input voltage, therefore the output and transmission matrices are :
\begin{align*}
	C_{SW}                 & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & 1 & 0 & 0
	\end{bmatrix} &
	D_{SW}                 & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Augmented System for Integral Action}
To ensure zero steady-state tracking error and robust disturbance rejection during hardware implementation, the standard 4-state model is augmented with a virtual fifth state representing the integral of the seesaw angle, 
\begin{equation*}
    \xi = \int \theta dt
\end{equation*}

The expanded state vector becomes $\eta = [x_c, \theta, \dot{x}_c, \dot{\theta}, \xi]^T$, producing the augmented matrices $A_5$ and $B_5$:
\begin{align*}
    A_5 &= \begin{bmatrix} A_{SW} & 0_{4\times1} \\ \begin{matrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{matrix} & 0 \end{bmatrix}, & B_5 &= \begin{bmatrix} B_{SW} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
This augmented model serves as the structural foundation for the State-Space techniques featuring an integral action.

\subsection{Seesaw Parameter Estimation}
While the manufacturer datasheet provides reliable nominal data for most of the parameters, the rotational dynamics are highly sensitive to the exact physical parameter $D_C$ (Distance from the pivot to the CoG). To mathematically lock this value, a data-driven Quasi-Static Lift-off Test was executed.\\
The procedure involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immobilizing the Cart: The cart is laid against the table at a known cart's position $x_{min} \simeq -\frac{T_c}{2} = -0.407[m]$ and seesaw's angle $\theta_{rest} \simeq e_{range} = 11.5[^\circ]$.
    \item Quasi-Static Actuation: A constant voltage is applied to the cart for it to reach velocity steady-state (i.e. $\ddot{x}_c = 0$), leaving only the gravitational and static torque balances active.
    \item Lift-Off Detection: A precise threshold filter algorithmically scans the telemetry data for the exact index where the angle breaks from $\theta_{rest}$ by more than 3 encoders ticks ($3 \cdot K_{E\_SW} / K_{gs} = 0.0015 \text{ rad}$). The cart's exact coordinate at this threshold is recorded as $x_{lift}$.
\end{itemize}
At the exact moment of lift-off, the static torques are perfectly balanced. The sum of moments about the pivot yields:
\begin{equation}
    g M_{tot} \cdot x_{lift} = g (M_{tot} D_T + M_{SW} D_C) \cdot \theta_{rest}
\end{equation}

Rearranging this equilibrium equation isolates the true center of gravity offset:
\begin{equation}
    D_C = \left| \frac{M_{tot} \cdot x_{lift} - M_{tot} D_T \cdot \theta_{rest}}{M_{sw} \cdot \theta_{rest}} \right|
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Stability-Driven Tuning}
Observing $G_\theta(s)$, there is two problems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The plant is a proper, but not strictly proper, transfer function. Meaning that the magnitude does not attenuate at higher frequency.
    \item It has both an unstable pole and an unstable zero, making it a \emph{Non-Minimum Phase} System.
\end{enumerate}
Separately the two instabilities, RHP pole and RHP zero, are stabilizable under the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item RHP pole in $p_0$ imposes a lower bound on the bandwidth (around $\omega_b > 2\cdot p_0$) because the controller must be fast enough to catch the system before it drifts away.
	\item RHP zero in $z_0$ imposes an upper bound on the bandwidth (around $\omega_b < \frac{z_0}{2}$) because $z_0$ makes the system "go in the wrong direction at first" (= undershoot)
\end{itemize}
Adapting it back to our unstable plant, 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The unstable pole in $p_0 = +2.7754 [rad/s]$ impose a minimal bandwidth of $\omega_b > 5.5508 [rad/s]$.
    \item The unstable zero in $z_0 = +8.8589[rad/s]$ imposes a maximal bandwidth of $\omega_b < 4.4295 [rad/s]$.
\end{itemize}
These limit values being theoretical, an attempt to control such system will still be performed.\\

\section{State-Space Control Design: Pole Placement}
\subsection{Full-State Feedback}
\label{sec:pole_placement}

Control law: $V_m = -\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}$, with closed-loop dynamics $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = (A - B\mathbf{K})\mathbf{x}$. The objective is to regulate $\theta \to 0$; cart motion serves only to maintain balance. On hardware, only $x_c$ and $\theta$ are measured; $\dot{x}_c$ and $\dot{\theta}$ are estimated via filtered derivatives.

\subsubsection{Open-Loop Poles}

The identified coupled plant, linearised about the horizontal equilibrium with the state ordered $[x_c,\ \theta,\ \dot{x}_c,\ \dot{\theta}]$, has open-loop poles
\begin{equation}

\subsection{State Estimator: Luenberger Observer}
Only $x_c$ and $\theta$ are measured; the two velocities $\dot{x}_c,\dot{\theta}$ in the feedback law must be reconstructed. Filtered differentiation of the encoders (the dirty derivative used in the loop) works but injects phase lag and amplifies quantization at high frequency. A Luenberger observer estimates the velocities from the plant model instead, and was logged alongside the dirty-derivative feedback as a benchmark.

\paragraph{Structure and separation.}
With measured outputs $y=C\mathbf{x}$, where $C=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ picks off $x_c$ and $\theta$, the observer
\begin{equation}
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{x}}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} + Bu + L\,(y - C\hat{\mathbf{x}})
\end{equation}
drives the estimation error $e=\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ by $\dot{e}=(A-LC)\,e$. Closing the loop with $u=-K_4\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ leaves a block-triangular system whose eigenvalues split as $\operatorname{eig}(A-BK_4)\cup\operatorname{eig}(A-LC)$ --- the separation principle. Controller and observer are therefore designed independently, and $L$ assigns the error poles freely because $(A,C)$ is observable: the observability matrix has full rank $4$ and condition number $211$, so both velocities are cleanly recoverable from the two position channels.

\paragraph{Pole selection.}
The observer poles are a proportional scaling of the controller poles --- the textbook $3$--$10\times$ rule for timescale separation, fixed at $k_{\mathrm{obs}}=5$ --- with two refinements. Only the \emph{real parts} are scaled, so the observer poles are all real and the estimate converges without ringing; and because the controller has a repeated real part ($-5.5$ twice) that pole placement cannot resolve, the duplicate is split by a small $\pm0.1$~rad/s offset. The result is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{obs}} = \{-27.4,\ -27.6,\ -41.2,\ -33.1\}\ \text{rad/s},
\end{equation}
the slowest pole a full decade above the unstable plant pole ($2.74$~rad/s) --- ample margin for the estimate to lead the regulator. The corresponding gain is
\begin{equation}
L = \begin{bmatrix} 46.4 & -0.9 \\ -4.3 & 68.5 \\ 247.1 & -20.5 \\ -197.9 & 1133.0 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why such an aggressive gain was acceptable.}
A fast observer is noisy: each encoder count maps to a voltage perturbation $|K_4 L|\,q$ at the motor. At $k_{\mathrm{obs}}=5$ this is $0.75$~V/count on the cart channel and $47$~V/count on the angle channel --- the latter far past the $6$~V saturation, so an \emph{in-loop} observer at this bandwidth would chatter the actuator into the rails and $k_{\mathrm{obs}}$ would have to be reduced. Here the observer ran only as a logged estimator while the dirty derivative drove the loop, so this noise constraint never bound; the aggressive bandwidth simply buys a sharp estimate to compare against. The optimal scaling could equally be found offline from logged voltage and position data.

\paragraph{Result.}
Logged against the encoders, the observer's angle innovation is $0.009^\circ$ RMS with near-white character (lag-1 correlation $0.82$), confirming a good match to the linear model. Its angular-rate estimate is visibly cleaner than the dirty derivative at high frequency, where the latter amplifies quantization (Fig.~\ref{fig:obs_pp}) --- the smoother velocity without the lag of an aggressively filtered differentiator that the observer promises, and the case for moving it into the loop next iteration at a noise-appropriate $k_{\mathrm{obs}}$.

\section{State-Space Control Design: Linear Quadratic Regulator}
Improvement from Pole Placement is LQR because it has guaranteed margins, in continuous time they are: gm inside (0.5 ; \inf) and pm of +- 60°.

\subsection{Full-State Feedback}
LQR is a full-state feedback optimization algorithm.
For the seesaw it translates to : instead of measuring the angle $\theta$ that we want to control, LQR assume to know the current state of everything in the system. It defines the feedback law as
\begin{align}\label{eq:LQR_FB_law_and_states}
	u & = -K \cdot \mathbf{x}, & \mathbf{x} & = \begin{bmatrix}
		                                            x_c \\ \theta \\ \dot{x_c} \\ \dot{\theta}
	                                            \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
LQR finds this optimal controller by minimizing a cost function $J$ based on Riccati's equation, see Appendix~\ref{app:ARE}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LQR_cost}
	J = \int _0 ^\infty (\mathbf{x}^\top \cdot Q \cdot \mathbf{x} +  \mathbf{u}^\top \cdot R \cdot \mathbf{u})dt
\end{equation}
The tuning parameters of LQR are the weight matrices Q and R, finding the balance between them defines how aggressive and "willing to spend more effort" the system will be.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Q} : The \emph{State Error} matrix (or Performance Penalty matrix) emphasize what state are important to control. A higher value $q_i$ means a more controlled state $i$, and a lower value means that any value can be accepted.
	      \begin{equation}
		      \mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix}
			      q_1 & 0   & 0   & 0   \\
			      0   & q_2 & 0   & 0   \\
			      0   & 0   & q_3 & 0   \\
			      0   & 0   & 0   & q_4 \\
		      \end{bmatrix}
		      \geq 0
	      \end{equation}
	\item \textbf{R} : The \emph{Input Error} matrix (or Effort Penalty matrix) penalizes the input states. As we only have one input for the seesaw, it is a scalar. A higher R means that the voltage must be lowered at all time, while a lower R tells the algorithm to use more power.
	      \begin{equation}
		      \mathbf{R} = r_1
	      \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
This solution gain $K$ can be interpreted, in standard algebra as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		V_m & = -K\cdot \mathbf{x}                                         \\
		       & = -(k_1 x_c + k_2 \theta + k_3 \dot{x}_c + k_4 \dot{\theta})
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This can easily be compared with what we were trying to do with the frequency-based technique, sec.~\ref{subsec:freq_based_control}, but this time instead of "guessing" how the cascade \emph{PD Controllers} should interact, the Riccati's Equation guarantees their balance.
\begin{align}
	V_m = \underbrace{(-k_1 x_c - k_3 \dot{x}_c)}_{\text{Cart PD Controller}} + \underbrace{(-k_2 \theta - k_4 \dot{\theta})}_{\text{Angle PD Controller}}
\end{align}
\paragraph{Conditions}
($A$,$B$) reachable, ($A$,$C_q$) observable with $Q = C_q'\cdot C_q$

\paragraph{Tuning}
There exists no mathematical way to find the optimal solution for LQR gains $Q$ and $R$, they must be found iteratively. However their behavior can be predicted.
Find best $Q$ and $R$ matrices : Bryson's Rule then iteratively

\paragraph{Feedback Problem}
We can only measure position and angle, but the feedback also requires velocity and angular velocity. Two options:
\begin{itemize}
	\item numerical derivative : works well in simulation but to avoid for real system since it is very subjective to sensor noise, and we use incremental sensor so it builds up.
	\item state estimators to create a State-Space of an observed system $\hat{x}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Integral Action}
This controller provides a relatively fast transient response and limited cart displacement, but it does not include integral action. Therefore, its ability to reject residual steady-state error caused by model mismatch or constant disturbances is inherently limited.

To improve regulation, an augmented 5-state LQR design was also introduced. An additional integral state is defined as
\[
	\xi = \int \theta(t)\,dt,
	\qquad
	\dot{\xi} = \theta,
\]
so that the augmented state vector becomes
\[
	\eta =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta} & \xi
	\end{bmatrix}^T.
\]

The corresponding augmented dynamics are written as
\[
	\dot{\eta} = A_5 \eta + B_5 u
\]
and the control law becomes
\[
	u = -K_5 \eta,
	\qquad
	K_5 = \mathrm{lqr}(A_5,B_5,Q_5,R_5).
\]

The augmented gain is obtained by minimizing the cost function
\[
	J_5 = \int_0^{\infty} \left( \eta^T Q_5 \eta + u^T R_5 u \right)\, dt.
\]

For this controller, the baseline weighting matrices were chosen as
\[
	Q_5 = \mathrm{diag}(1000,\;5000,\;0,\;0,\;2000),
	\qquad
	R_5 = 0.5.
\]
The zero weights on the two velocities follow Bryson's rule: only the cart position, the angle and the integral state carry a ``desired magnitude'', the velocities being penalized only indirectly through the dynamics. Solving the Riccati equation with these weights gives the deployed gain (state ordered $[x_c,\ \theta,\ \dot{x}_c,\ \dot{\theta},\ \xi]$)
\begin{equation}
	K_5 = \begin{bmatrix} 191.9 & -275.5 & 14.5 & -92.8 & -63.2 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
whose loop, broken at the plant input, has a $63.7^\circ$ phase margin and crossover at $10.8$~rad/s --- comfortably inside the $\pm60^\circ$/$(0.5,\infty)$ margins the LQR guarantees in continuous time.

The augmented system is also fully controllable, with
\[
	\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{C}_5 = 5.
\]
As expected, the open-loop augmented model contains an additional pole at the origin due to the integral state. After feedback is applied, all closed-loop poles lie in the stable region, confirming that the augmented controller also stabilizes the system. 

Compared with the 4-gain design, the augmented controller introduces integral action and is therefore better suited to reducing residual regulation error in the presence of non-ideal effects.

The role of the weighting matrices follows the standard LQR interpretation. Increasing the weights associated with $x_c$ and $\theta$ gives higher priority to cart-position and angle regulation, respectively. Increasing the weight on the integral state $\xi$ strengthens the long-term correction of angular deviation and generally improves regulation speed, although it may also make the controller more aggressive. On the other hand, increasing $R$ penalizes the control input more heavily, resulting in a more conservative controller with smoother actuation but slower closed-loop dynamics.

\textcolor{blue}{second option}\\

To further improve regulation and tracking performance, an LQI (\textit{Linear Quadratic Integral}) controller was also considered. The LQI approach extends the standard LQR design by augmenting the system with an integral state associated with the output error. In this way, the controller not only stabilizes the system, but also reduces or eliminates steady-state error in the presence of constant disturbances, modeling inaccuracies, or nonzero reference commands.

For the Seesaw system, the controlled output is chosen as the angular position $\theta$. Defining the regulation error as
\[
	e(t)=r(t)-\theta(t),
\]
where $r(t)$ is the reference angle, the integral state is introduced as
\[
	\xi(t)=\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]
For the regulation problem considered in this work, the reference is $r(t)=0$, so the error reduces to
\[
	e(t)=-\theta(t),
\]
and therefore
\[
	\dot{\xi}(t)=-\theta(t).
\]

Using the original state vector
\[
	x=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta}
	\end{bmatrix}^T,
\]
the augmented state vector becomes
\[
	x_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x \\
		\xi
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_c & \theta & \dot{x}_c & \dot{\theta} & \xi
	\end{bmatrix}^T.
\]

The augmented system can then be written as
\[
	\dot{x}_a=A_a x_a+B_a u+B_r r
\]
with
\[
	A_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		A         & 0 \\
		-C_\theta & 0
	\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	B_a=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		B \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	B_r=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		1
	\end{bmatrix},
\]
where
\[
	C_\theta=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & 1 & 0 & 0
	\end{bmatrix}
\]
selects the angular state.

The LQI gain is obtained by minimizing the quadratic cost function
\[
	J_{\mathrm{LQI}}
	=
	\int_0^\infty
	\left(
	x_a^T Q_a x_a + u^T R u
	\right)\,dt
\]
where $Q_a \succeq 0$ weights both the physical states and the integral state, while $R \succ 0$ penalizes the control effort. The optimal control law is
\[
	u=-K_a x_a
	=
	-\begin{bmatrix}
		K_x & K_i
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x \\
		\xi
	\end{bmatrix}.
\]

In this formulation, the matrix $K_x$ acts on the original plant states, while the scalar gain $K_i$ acts on the integral state. The integral action continuously accumulates the angle error and reinforces the control input until the steady-state deviation is removed. For this reason, the LQI controller is theoretically more effective than a standard LQR controller whenever constant disturbances, parameter uncertainty, or reference changes are present.\\

Finally, it should be emphasized that the actuator voltage is limited to $\pm 6~\mathrm{V}$. Under saturation, the integral state may continue to accumulate error even when the actuator can no longer provide additional effort. This phenomenon, known as integral windup, may degrade performance and should be addressed in a practical implementation through an anti-windup strategy. Nevertheless, the simulation results confirm that the LQI controller stabilizes the Seesaw system and improves angular regulation compared with the standard LQR design.

\paragraph{Design intent versus measured behaviour.}
The linear designs all aim for a well-damped response: pole placement puts the balancing pair at $1.09$~Hz with $\zeta=0.8$ (predicting $\approx1.5\%$ overshoot and a $0.7$~s settle), and the LQR crosses over near $1.7$~Hz with a $64^\circ$ phase margin. The hardware does not match this on-paper damping. Every controller overshoots its $1^\circ$ step far more than designed --- to $2.1$--$3.6^\circ$ --- and the swept-sine magnitude shows a clear resonant peak ($3.4\times$ for pole placement, $4.2\times$ for the LQR, $6.0\times$ for the PID). The time-domain overshoot and the frequency-domain peak are two independent measurements of the same thing, and they agree: the closed loop is markedly more lightly damped than the pole locations claim.

This is also why the swept sine earns its place despite the small drive amplitude ($0.2^\circ$, below the $0.3$--$0.7^\circ$ limit cycle): the contamination affects only the \emph{absolute low-frequency gain}, and hence the precise $-3$~dB point, which is why the bandwidth above is read as a lower bound. The \emph{shape} of the response --- the resonant peak that quantifies the under-damping, the roll-off, the phase loss and the controller ranking --- sits well clear of the rocking floor and corroborates the step data.

The damping shortfall is common to all three controllers, so it is a property of the plant-plus-implementation rather than a mis-tuning of one design. Three mechanisms account for it, none of them in the linear model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dirty-derivative phase lag}: the velocities are reconstructed by filtered differentiation rather than the ideal full-state measurement the design assumes, spending phase margin that shows up as reduced damping.
    \item \textbf{Coulomb friction, stiction and gear backlash}: visible directly as the $1.5$--$2^\circ$ staircase hysteresis (Fig.~\ref{fig:staircase}) and the sustained limit cycle at $\theta_{ref}=0$.
    \item \textbf{Encoder quantization}, which sustains the residual rocking and sets the floor below which small-signal tracking cannot be resolved.
\end{itemize}
The claims that survive this scrutiny are the ones measured well clear of these effects: each controller stabilizes the unstable plant, tracks to a near-zero steady-state error through integral action, holds a tight regulation band ($0.32$--$0.74^\circ$ RMS), and, for the LQR, tracks cleanly up to $10^\circ$. The precise damping ratio and absolute bandwidth are best read as indicative.

\section{LQR and Riccati's Equation}\label{app:ARE}
LQR finds the full-state feedback control law $u = -\bar{K}x$.\\
The algebraic Riccati's equation is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ARE}
	\left\{
	\begin{aligned}
		Q       & - \bar{P}BR^{-1}B^\top\bar{P} + \bar{P}A + A^\top \bar{P} = 0 \\
		\dot{P} & (t) = P(t) = \bar{P}
	\end{aligned}
	\right.
\end{equation}
With $\bar{P}$ symmetric unique positive definite solution of ARE.\\
The gain is given by :
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ARE_gain}
	\bar{K} = R^{-1}B^\top \bar{P}
\end{equation}
